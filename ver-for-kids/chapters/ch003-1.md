# 儿童道德经第三章：其一 把闪亮的东西收一收

## 原文

### 第1页

不尚贤，使民不争；

不贵难得之货，使民不为盗；

不见可欲，使民心不乱。

### 第2页

是以圣人之治，虚其心，实其腹，弱其志，强其骨。

常使民无知无欲。使夫知者不敢为也。

为无为，则无不治。

## 释义

### 第3页

“不尚贤”针对的是一块招牌。有本领是好事；把“最聪明”“最厉害”做成高高的招牌挂起来，人人都抬头去看，脚下就要挤起来。

本领用在事情上，事情会变好。本领用在比高低上，人就只想压过旁边的人。

### 第4页

稀奇的东西可以喜欢，可以使用。“不贵难得之货”说的是别把它摆到最高的地方、再打上灯照着，让一屋子人的心只剩一句“我要”。

心被勾着跑久了，手就会伸到不该伸的地方去。

### 第5页

“虚其心”，是把心里比来比去的念头清出一点空处。“实其腹”，是让人吃饱、睡稳，有力气过日子。

“弱其志”，是把总想赢过别人的那股劲慢慢柔下来；“强其骨”，是把身体养得结实。

## 难词注解

### 第6页

“尚贤”是把有本事的人捧成一块高高的招牌。老子要说的是这块招牌挂得太高，他并不排斥真有本事的人。

“争”是抢第一、抢位置。心里只剩“我要赢”，人就会挤起来。

### 第7页

“难得之货”指少见又贵重的东西，也指权力、名声和特殊的位置。

“可欲”指一看见就让人心里发痒的东西。喜欢它没有错，心跟着它跑才会乱。

### 第8页

“虚其心”是让心留出空处，不让乱七八糟的念头把心塞满。

“实其腹”是让人吃饱睡好，安安稳稳过日子。

“无知无欲”是少一点小心眼和贪心，守住简单和真实。

“为无为”是该做的做，不添自己的私心。

## 正文

### 第9页

空空村要办秋日会。阿禾写了三块大牌子：最聪明、最快、故事讲得最好。

牌子还没挂上去，孩子们已经围过来问：“我能不能得第一？”“第一名有没有红绳子？”“要是我输了怎么办？”

### 第10页

小兔半夜起来练跑步，听见有人走近，赶紧躲到树后。小石头把故事背了七遍，临到开口，舌头先打了结。小鸟从早喊到晚，嗓子哑了，晚饭也没吃出味道。

老人看了看那三块牌子，说：“这些字太亮了，把人的眼睛都照到名次上去了。”

### 第11页

阿禾又拿出一盒彩石，要摆到最高的那张桌子上。盒盖一开，石头在日光里亮起来，一屋子小孩都不出声了。

孩子们伸长脖子，脚尖踮得高高的。小兔问能不能摸一下，小石头问谁能带回家，旁边有人说了两遍“让一让”，谁也没听见。

### 第12页

老人把彩石收进布袋，扎紧袋口，又把最高的桌子搬走，换上平时吃饭的长桌。

桌上摆着热粥、青菜和馒头。孩子们坐下来，眼睛还跟着布袋走。粥喝到一半，手就放回膝盖上了。

### 第13页

吃完饭，老人把木条、绳子和小锤子放在地上。地上还有一座被雨冲歪的小桥，等着人来扶正。

小兔量绳，小鸟递木条，小石头敲钉。阿禾发现，各人管住手上一截，小桥很快就站稳了。

## 回扣

### 第14页

第三章讲的是：名声挂得高，人就争；宝贝摆得亮，人就贪；欲望被挑起来，心就乱。

好东西留着用。要收的是多余的火。火太旺，饭会烧焦；火稳一点，粥才慢慢熟。

### 第15页

阿禾在本子上写：把肚子填饱，把骨头养稳，把心里那句“我一定要赢”放轻。

大家不忙着争名头，手就回到要做的事情上。秋日会那天，小桥稳稳地立着，粥是热的。

# Chapter 003-1: Put the Shiny Things Away

## Original Text

### Page 1

Not to value and employ men of superior ability is the way to keep the people from rivalry among themselves; not to prize articles which are difficult to procure is the way to keep them from becoming thieves; not to show them what is likely to excite their desires is the way to keep their minds from disorder.

### Page 2

Therefore the sage, in the exercise of his government, empties their minds, fills their bellies, weakens their wills, and strengthens their bones.

He constantly (tries to) keep them without knowledge and without desire, and where there are those who have knowledge, to keep them from presuming to act (on it). When there is this abstinence from action, good order is universal.

## Interpretation

### Page 3

"Not to value men of superior ability" points at a public habit. Skill is a good thing; hang "the smartest" and "the greatest" on a high signboard, and everyone looks up, and the feet below begin to shove.

Put skill into the work, and the work gets better. Put skill into the ranking, and people think only of standing above the person beside them.

### Page 4

Rare things may be enjoyed and used. "Not to prize articles which are difficult to procure" means keeping them off the highest table, out of the lamp's light, so that a whole room of hearts does not narrow to one sentence: "I want that."

A heart pulled along long enough will send the hand after what belongs to someone else.

### Page 5

"Empties their minds" clears the busy comparing out of the heart and leaves a little room. "Fills their bellies" helps people eat and sleep well and live their days in peace.

"Weakens their wills" softens the urge to win over others at any cost. "Strengthens their bones" makes the body strong and steady.

## Vocabulary Notes

### Page 6

The phrase “value and employ men of superior ability” is the translator Legge's way of describing the public honoring of talented people. Laozi's warning aims at the signboard hung too high; he does not push away people of real skill.

The word “rivalry” means fighting to be first or to hold the better place. When the heart says only “I must win,” people start to shove.

### Page 7

The phrase “articles which are difficult to procure” means rare and costly things that people chase. It can also stand for power, rank, or a special place.

The phrase “excite their desires” means making people want something so much that they forget what matters.

### Page 8

The phrase “empties their minds” means not letting a mess of thoughts fill the heart.

The phrase “fills their bellies” means eating well, sleeping well, and living steady days.

The phrase “without knowledge and without desire” means less cleverness and less greed, keeping to what is simple and true.

The phrase “abstinence from action” means acting without selfish forcing.

## Main Text

### Page 9

Kongkong Village was preparing for the Autumn Gathering. Ahe wrote three big signs: Smartest, Fastest, Best Storyteller.

Before the signs were even hung, the children crowded around. "Can I win first?" "Will first place get a red cord?" "What if I lose?"

### Page 10

Little Rabbit got up in the night to practice running, and hid behind a tree when someone came near. Little Stone memorized the story seven times, and at the first line his tongue tangled. Little Bird called out from morning to night until her throat hurt and dinner had no taste.

The old man looked at the three signs. "These words are too bright," he said. "They shine everyone's eyes onto ranking."

### Page 11

Then Ahe brought out a box of colored stones and carried it toward the highest table. When the lid opened, the stones caught the daylight and the whole room went quiet.

The children stretched their necks and stood on tiptoe. Little Rabbit asked to touch one; Little Stone asked who could take one home. Someone beside them said "please make room" twice, and no one heard.

### Page 12

The old man put the stones into a cloth bag and pulled the drawstring tight. He moved the high table away and brought in the long table they ate from every day.

On it were warm porridge, greens, and buns. The children sat down with their eyes still on the bag. Halfway through the porridge, their hands went back to their knees.

### Page 13

After dinner, the old man laid wood, rope, and a small hammer on the ground. Beside them stood a little bridge the rain had pushed crooked, waiting to be set right.

Little Rabbit measured rope, Little Bird passed wood, and Little Stone tapped nails. Ahe noticed that when each pair of hands took its own length of the work, the bridge stood steady very soon.

## Return to the Original

### Page 14

Chapter Three says: hang a name high, and people fight for it; set treasure out bright, and people grow greedy; stir up desire, and the heart loses its footing.

Good things can stay. What needs lowering is the extra fire. Too much flame burns the rice; a steady flame lets the porridge slowly cook.

### Page 15

Ahe wrote in his notebook: Fill the belly, steady the bones, and set the thought "I must win" down a little.

When no one is busy fighting for a title, hands come back to the work that needs doing. On the day of the Autumn Gathering, the little bridge stood straight and the porridge was hot.
