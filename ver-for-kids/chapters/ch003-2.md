# 儿童道德经第三章：其二 青牛和千里马

## 第1页

传说老子离开周朝都城时，没有坐华丽的大车。他骑着一头青牛，慢慢走向函谷关。

青牛的步子慢，样子也普通。可是那条慢慢的路，很像老子说的“不贵难得之货”：不把闪亮的东西摆在心的中央。

到了关口，守关的官员尹喜请他留下几句话。老子写下五千言，然后继续往西去。一卷书，一条向西的路，这就是他留下的全部。

## 第2页

汉文帝也有一个故事。有人献上一匹千里马，说它一天能跑很远。

文帝没有马上收下。他问：我的车队一天只能走几十里，我一个人骑得再快，又能先到哪里去呢？

他把马退还，还给献马的人路费，又告诉四方：以后不要再送这种稀奇东西。文帝懂好马。他不想让天下人都追着“难得之货”跑。

## 第3页

空空村来了一个卖马人，牵着一匹“风一样快”的小马。他摇着铜铃喊：“谁买到它，谁就是全村最神气的人！”

孩子们全挤到路边，眼睛跟着小马转。地上的豆苗被踩弯了，也没人发现。

## 第4页

阿林也想骑那匹小马。他想象自己骑着小马冲过村口，大家都喊：“阿林最厉害！”

这个念头刚冒出来，铜铃一响，他的脚就跟着往前挪。

## 第5页

老人牵来村里的老牛。老牛走得慢，眼睛湿润，鼻子上还沾着草屑。

阿林小声说：“它一点也不像宝贝。”老人摸摸牛背：“昨天大家的米袋，就是它一袋一袋拉回来的。”

## 第6页

小马很快，却只能载一个人。老牛很慢，却能拉一车粮食，也能把柴送到每家门口。

阿林忽然明白：有用的东西，常常站在最低的地方，安安静静把活干完。

## 第7页

卖马人又摇铜铃。小兔往前挤，小石头也往前挤，眼看就要吵起来。

老人请卖马人走远一点再摇铃，又让孩子们去扶起被踩弯的豆苗。铃声一远，路边才慢慢安静。

## 第8页

傍晚，老牛拖着车，把木柴送到每家门口。木柴靠上门边，它就往下一家走。

每一家的炉火都亮了。阿林觉得，这种暖，比“最神气”三个字踏实多了。

## 第9页

第二天，阿林把那盒彩石拿出来。它们还是很美，只是不再摆在最高处。

他把彩石分给大家用：大的压住书角，小的立在菜地边，扁的摆在路口当记号。彩石落到大家手里，每一颗都派上了用场。

## 第10页

老人说：“珍贵的东西可以用，也可以欣赏，只是别让它牵着人心跑。”

阿林想起老子的青牛，也想起汉文帝退回的千里马。大家眼里若只剩宝贝，饭、路、豆苗和身边的人，就容易被忘掉。

## 第11页

“不尚贤”也是这样。真正有本领的人，常常像青牛慢慢走路、老牛送柴那样，把该做的事做好，不必站到台上让大家夸。

名头越亮，心越容易乱。事情越实在，村子越容易安。

## 第12页

别把最快的马放在心的正中央。也要看见慢慢走的牛，看见每一袋米、每一束柴。

心不追着铃声跑，村子就能听见自己的脚步。

# Chapter 003-2: The Blue-Green Ox and the Thousand-Li Horse

## Page 1

Legend says that when Laozi left the Zhou capital, he did not ride in a splendid carriage. He rode a blue-green ox slowly toward Hangu Pass.

The ox was plain, and its steps were slow. Yet that slow road matches Laozi's teaching: do not place shiny, hard-to-get things at the center of the heart.

At the pass, Yin Xi, the keeper of the pass, asked Laozi to leave some words behind. Laozi wrote five thousand characters, then continued west. One scroll, and one road west — that was all he left behind.

## Page 2

Emperor Wen of Han has another story. Someone offered him a horse said to travel a thousand li in a day.

The emperor did not accept it at once. He asked: if my procession can only move a few dozen li a day, where could I go first by riding alone on the fastest horse?

He returned the horse, paid the giver's travel costs, and told the land not to send such rare gifts again. He understood good horses. He simply did not want everyone chasing rare goods.

## Page 3

A horse seller came to Kongkong Village with a "wind-fast" pony. He rang a bronze bell and called, "Whoever buys it will be the grandest person in the village!"

The children crowded by the road, their eyes following the pony. Nobody noticed the bean sprouts being stepped flat.

## Page 4

Lin wanted to ride the pony too. He imagined riding through the village gate while everyone shouted, "Lin is the greatest!"

The thought had barely appeared when the bell rang, and his feet moved toward it.

## Page 5

The old man led out the village ox. It walked slowly, with damp eyes and bits of grass on its nose.

"It does not look like treasure," Lin whispered. The old man patted the ox's back. "Yesterday it brought everyone's rice sacks home, one by one."

## Page 6

The pony was fast, but it could carry only one person. The ox was slow, but it could pull a cart of grain and carry firewood to each door.

Lin suddenly understood: useful things often stand in the lowest places, quietly finishing the work.

## Page 7

The seller rang the bell again. Little Rabbit pushed forward, Little Stone pushed too, and everyone nearly argued.

The old man asked the seller to walk farther off before ringing the bell. Then he sent the children to lift the bent bean sprouts. As the bell faded, the roadside slowly quieted.

## Page 8

At dusk, the ox pulled a cart and delivered firewood to each door. The wood leaned against each doorstep, then the ox moved on.

Every stove was lit. To Lin, that warmth felt much steadier than being called grand.

## Page 9

The next day, Lin brought out the box of colored stones. They were still beautiful, but he no longer placed them on the highest table.

He shared them out: large ones held book corners, small ones stood by the garden beds, flat ones became signs at the path. In people's hands, each stone found a use.

## Page 10

The old man said, "Precious things may be used and admired. Just do not let them pull the heart along."

Lin thought of Laozi's blue-green ox and the thousand-li horse Emperor Wen returned. When people see only treasure, they easily forget food, roads, bean sprouts, and the people beside them.

## Page 11

"Not to value superior ability" is similar. A truly capable person can work like the blue-green ox walking slowly or the village ox delivering firewood, doing the task at hand without standing on a stage for praise.

The brighter the title, the easier the heart grows restless. The more real the work, the calmer the village becomes.

## Page 12

Do not put the fastest horse at the center of your heart. Notice the slow ox too, every sack of rice, and every bundle of wood.

When the heart stops running after bells, the village can hear its own footsteps.
