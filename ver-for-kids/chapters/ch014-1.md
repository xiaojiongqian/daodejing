# 儿童道德经第十四章：其一 摸不着的一

## 第1页

视之不见名曰夷，听之不闻名曰希，搏之不得名曰微。
此三者，不可致诘，故混而为一。

其上不皦，其下不昧。
绳绳不可名，复归于无物。

## 第2页

是谓无状之状，无物之象，是谓惚恍。

迎之不见其首，随之不见其后。

执古之道，以御今之有。
能知古始，是谓道纪。

## 第3页

第十四章讲一种很难抓住的东西。看，看不见；听，听不到；抓，抓不住，可它并非空无一物，花草、石头、人，好像都从它那里得到自己的样子。

“夷”“希”“微”最后合在一起，成为“一”。

## 第4页

这个“一”不会站成物件，让人看、让人听、让人拿。

“迎之不见其首，随之不见其后”，像追一阵风：迎面看不见开头，追上去也看不见末尾。树叶动了，水面皱了，你知道它来过。

## 第5页

“执古之道，以御今之有”，说的是有些老道理，今天仍能帮我们处理眼前的事，并非要人死守旧东西。

水往低处流，种子要土和时节，人心太满就听不进话。这些道理很老，却像每天的太阳一样新。

## 第6页

“夷”本来有平而无形的意思，这里指看不见。“希”本来有稀少、细微的意思，这里指听不清或听不见。“微”是又小又细，深得抓不住。

“不可致诘”是不能像说明普通物件那样逐层问清。“惚恍”是似有似无、抓不牢。“道纪”是道的线索，像一卷线的线头。

## 第7页

阿林想画一幅“道”的图。他铺开纸，磨好墨，先画一条路，画完又摇头：“路只是路，不是道。”

他又画一条溪。溪水弯弯绕绕，很像前几章说过的水。可他想了想，还是摇头：“水像道，却也不是道的全部。”

## 第8页

小石头跑来：“画风吧，风看不见，最像。”阿林拿笔点了半天，只画出几片斜斜的叶子。

“风自己画不出来。”小石头说。老人从旁边走过：“画不出风，不等于风没有来。”

## 第9页

老人带他们到晒谷场。

谷粒摊在竹席上，太阳照着，风轻轻过来。阿林看不见风的头，也抓不到风的尾，可谷壳被吹到一边，稻香从席面浮起来。

## 第10页

他们又走到老水渠旁。

这条水渠是很久以前的村民挖的，贴着地势绕过石头，低处接水，高处不硬冲。去年大雨时，新修的直沟被冲坏，老水渠还稳稳把水送到田边。

## 第11页

老人说：“这就叫旧道理管今天的事。”

“古人不知道我们的鞋子、灯和纸风车，可他们知道水性、土性、人心。这些道理，今天还是一样的。”

## 第12页

阿林蹲在水渠边，看见水沿着旧痕往前走。

旧痕不是水，水也不是道。可是那种“顺着地势、不硬拧”的道理，从很久以前走到今天，仍然有用。

## 第13页

傍晚，阿林回到纸前，画了一只转动的车轮、一口空罐、一扇开着的窗、一条弯曲的水渠，还有几片被风吹起的谷壳。

小石头问：“这就是道吗？”阿林说：“不是。可是它们像道留下的脚印。”

## 第14页

第十四章说“视之不见，听之不闻，搏之不得”。风看不见，谷壳会动；道摸不着，万物却顺着它生长变化。

“混而为一”并不要人把眼睛、耳朵和手弄糊涂。它提醒我们，世界还有更深的线索，藏在事情怎样发生、怎样变化里面。

## 第15页

“执古之道，以御今之有”，是从古老的道理里找到活的线索，照看今天的水渠、心和要做的事，并非抱着旧东西不放。

看不见的，不一定空；抓不住的，不一定无用。看见脚印，就知道有路；看见万物各归其所，就知道道在悄悄经过。

# Chapter 014-1: The One That Cannot Be Held

## Page 1

We look at it, and we do not see it, and we name it 'the Equable.'  We listen to it, and we do not hear it, and we name it 'the Inaudible.'  We try to grasp it, and do not get hold of it, and we name it 'the Subtle.'  With these three qualities, it cannot be made the subject of description; and hence we blend them together and obtain The One.

Its upper part is not bright, and its lower part is not obscure.  Ceaseless in its action, it yet cannot be named, and then it again returns and becomes nothing.

## Page 2

This is called the Form of the Formless, and the Semblance of the Invisible; this is called the Fleeting and Indeterminable.

We meet it and do not see its Front; we follow it, and do not see its Back.  When we can lay hold of the Tao of old to direct the things of the present day, and are able to know it as it was of old in the beginning, this is called (unwinding) the clue of Tao.

## Page 3

Chapter Fourteen speaks of something very hard to grasp. We look and do not see it, listen and do not hear it, reach out and cannot hold it. Yet it is not simply nothing: flowers, stones, and people all seem to get their shape from it.

The Equable, the Inaudible, and the Subtle finally blend together and become the One.

## Page 4

This One does not stand before us as an object to see, hear, or hold.

"We meet it and do not see its front; we follow it and do not see its back" is like chasing wind. Face it, and you cannot see where it begins. Run after it, and you cannot see where it ends. Yet leaves move and water wrinkles, so you know it has passed.

## Page 5

"Holding the Tao of old to direct what exists today" means that some old truths still help with present matters. It does not ask us to cling to old things.

Water flows downward, seeds need soil and season, and a heart too full cannot hear. These truths are old, and as new as the sun each morning.

## Page 6

“Equable” means even and without a visible form. “Inaudible” means unable to be heard. “Subtle” means so fine, small, and deep that the hand cannot hold it.

“Cannot be made the subject of description” means it cannot be explained piece by piece like an ordinary object. “Fleeting and indeterminable” means seeming to be there and not there. “Clue of Tao” is a guiding thread, like the loose end of a spool.

## Page 7

Lin wanted to draw a picture of the Dao. He spread paper, ground ink, and first drew a road. When he finished, he shook his head. "A road is only a road, not the Dao."

Then he drew a stream. It curved and turned, much like the water from earlier chapters. But after thinking, he shook his head again. "Water is like the Dao, but not the whole Dao."

## Page 8

Little Stone ran over. "Draw wind. Wind cannot be seen, so it is most like it." Lin worked the brush for a long time but produced only a few slanting leaves.

"Wind itself cannot be drawn," Little Stone said. The old man passed by. "You cannot draw the wind. The wind came anyway."

## Page 9

He brought them to the grain-drying ground.

Grains lay spread on bamboo mats. Sunlight shone, and wind came softly. Lin could not see the wind's head or grasp its tail. Yet husks blew aside and the scent of rice rose from the mat.

## Page 10

They walked on to the old irrigation ditch.

Long ago, villagers had dug it along the land's slope. It curved around stones, received water in low places, and did not force itself through high places. Last year, heavy rain broke a new straight ditch, yet the old ditch still carried water to the field.

## Page 11

The old man said, "This is an old truth guiding today's matter."

"The old villagers knew nothing of our shoes, lamps, or paper pinwheels. They knew water, soil, and the human heart. These truths are still the same today."

## Page 12

Lin crouched by the ditch and watched the water follow the old marks.

The marks were not water, and water was not the Dao. Yet the principle of following the land and not forcing it had walked from long ago into today, still useful.

## Page 13

At dusk, Lin returned to his paper. He drew a turning wheel, an empty jar, an open window, a curving ditch, and a few husks lifted by wind.

Little Stone asked, "Is this the Dao?" Lin said, "No. But they are like footprints it leaves."

## Page 14

Chapter Fourteen says that we cannot see it, hear it, or grasp it. Wind is unseen, yet husks move. The Dao cannot be held, yet things grow and change by it.

"Blending into one" does not ask us to confuse our eyes, ears, and hands. It reminds us that the world has deeper threads, hidden in how things happen and change.

## Page 15

"Holding the Tao of old to direct what exists today" means finding living clues in ancient truth and using them to care for today's ditch, heart, and work. It does not mean clutching old objects.

What cannot be seen is not always empty; what cannot be held is not always useless. Seeing footprints tells us there is a road. Seeing things find their place tells us the Dao has quietly passed.
