# 儿童道德经第五章：其一 空空的风箱

## 第1页

天地不仁，以万物为刍狗；
圣人不仁，以百姓为刍狗。

## 第2页

天地之间，其犹橐籥乎？
虚而不屈，动而愈出。
多言数穷，不如守中。

## 第3页

“不仁”是天地让万物自己生长、归去，不按人的好恶安排。王弼解释说，这不是冷酷，也不是叫人不爱人。雨落下来，不会先问哪朵花最乖；风吹过去，也不会只吹给谁一个人。

“刍狗”是古代祭礼里用草扎成的狗。祭礼前，人们认真摆好；祭礼结束，它就用完了，回到草木里去。老子借它说，天地不会把哪样东西永远捧在手心。

## 第4页

圣人也学天地，不把“我多仁慈”挂在嘴边。该扶的时候扶，该止的时候止，不让偏爱挡住眼睛。

真正的照顾，是看清事情怎样才能好好地继续下去，不靠一时心热，乱答应、乱帮忙。

## 第5页

“橐籥”像古代风箱，中间空空的。手一推一拉，风就送进炉膛，火也更旺。

河上公把这一章叫“虚用”。空让风有路；中间不被塞满，力量才能源源不断出来。

## 第6页

“仁”在这里指带着人情偏爱的“我想怎样对你”，跟日常的善良不同。“刍狗”是草扎的祭礼之物，不会因谁喜欢就被偏爱。“橐籥”是古代风箱，靠中间的空处和来回推拉送风。

“虚而不屈”是空着却用不完；“动而愈出”是一动起来，作用更多；“守中”是守住中心和分寸，不被话语、情绪和偏见拉偏。

## 第7页

铁匠铺里有一只旧风箱。它肚子空空，木板被摸得发亮，手柄也磨得很光。

小徒弟问：“师父，空东西怎么能让炉火变旺？”

## 第8页

师父拉一下，风从管口冲进炉膛。火苗先低了一低，马上又亮起来。

他再推一下，炭火红得像星子。小徒弟睁大眼：原来，这么大的风，是从空肚子里来的。

## 第9页

小徒弟拿来一团棉布，想塞进风箱。“塞满了，风就不会乱跑了吧？”

师父笑着摇头：“塞满了，它就不再会呼吸。”

## 第10页

傍晚下雨，雨点落在屋顶，也落在泥地、石头和野草上。最不起眼的那朵野花，也接到了水。

阿林站在门口看了很久，忽然觉得：天地待每朵花都一样，这比只疼一朵花大得多。

## 第11页

第二天，孩子们争着解释风箱。有人说它神奇，有人说它普通，声音越挤越急。

阿林把手放在风箱边，等风吹过，才轻轻说：“它中间空，所以风能过。”老人点点头：“话说到点子上，心里就有地方。”

孩子们安静下来，只听见风箱一推一拉，像天地在慢慢呼吸。

## 第12页

第五章说，天地按自己的规律运行，不凭私心给谁加糖、给谁下雨。它不记谁乖谁不乖的账，也不是冷酷到不管万物。

真正的爱，不忙着夸自己，也不被偏心塞满。像风箱守着中间的空，该动时动，该停时停。心中有空，风才不断；守住中心，话和行动才不乱。

# Chapter 005-1: The Empty Bellows

## Page 1

Heaven and earth do not act from (the impulse of) any wish to be benevolent; they deal with all things as the dogs of grass are dealt with.

The sages do not act from (any wish to be) benevolent; they deal with the people as the dogs of grass are dealt with.

## Page 2

May not the space between heaven and earth be compared to a bellows?

'Tis emptied, yet it loses not its power;
'Tis moved again, and sends forth air the more.

Much speech to swift exhaustion lead we see;
Your inner being guard, and keep it free.

## Page 3

Here, "not benevolent" means heaven and earth let things grow and go by themselves, without arranging the world by human likes and dislikes. Wang Bi explains that this is not cruelty, nor a command to stop loving. Rain does not ask which flower is best behaved; wind does not blow for only one person.

"Straw dogs" were grass figures used in old rites. People set them out with care before the rite; afterward, their use was over, and they returned to the grass. Laozi uses them to show that heaven and earth do not hold any one thing forever in their hands.

## Page 4

The sage learns from heaven and earth. He does not hang a sign saying, "Look how kind I am." He helps when help is needed, stops when stopping is needed, and does not let favorites cover his eyes.

True care sees how life can go on well, and it does not come from a sudden hot feeling that promises and helps everywhere.

## Page 5

A bellows is an old tool with a hollow middle. A push and a pull send air into the furnace, and the fire burns brighter.

The old commentator Heshang Gong called this chapter "the use of emptiness." Empty space gives the wind a path; when the middle stays clear, strength can keep coming out.

## Page 6

"Any wish to be benevolent" means partial human love: "I will treat you the way I want to." It differs from everyday kindness. "Dogs of grass" were grass figures for ancient rites; their treatment did not depend on whether someone liked or hated them. A "bellows" is an old tool that sends air into a fire.

"'Tis emptied, yet it loses not its power" means empty space lets breath keep moving. "'Tis moved again, and sends forth air the more" means movement brings more air. "Your inner being guard, and keep it free" means staying steady, not pulled aside by words, fear, or bias.

## Page 7

In the blacksmith's shop, there was an old bellows. Its belly was hollow, its wood shone from years of touch, and its handle was smooth.

The apprentice asked, "Master, how can an empty thing make the fire stronger?"

## Page 8

The master pulled once, and wind rushed into the furnace. The flame dipped, then rose brighter.

He pushed again, and the coals glowed like stars. The apprentice stared: such a strong wind had come from a hollow belly.

## Page 9

The apprentice brought a wad of cotton and wanted to stuff it into the bellows. "If we fill it, the wind will not run around, right?"

The master smiled and shook his head. "If we fill it, it will no longer breathe."

## Page 10

At dusk, rain fell on the roof, the mud, the stones, and the wild grass. Even the plainest wildflower caught its share.

Lin stood at the door for a long time. Heaven and earth treat every flower the same way, he thought, and that is bigger than loving just one flower.

## Page 11

The next day, the children argued about the bellows. One called it magic, another called it ordinary, and their voices crowded together.

Lin placed a hand beside it, waited for the wind, and then said softly, "It is hollow in the middle, so the wind can pass." The old man nodded. "When words go to the point, the heart has room."

The children grew quiet. They only heard the bellows push and pull, like heaven and earth breathing slowly.

## Page 12

Chapter Five shows heaven and earth moving by their own patterns, never adding sugar or rain out of private feeling. They keep no ledger of who is good and who is not, and they are not so cold that they forget all things.

True love does not rush to praise itself, and it is not stuffed full of favorites. Like a bellows keeping its hollow center, it moves when needed and rests when needed. When the heart has room, the wind keeps coming; when the center is kept, speech and action do not scatter.
