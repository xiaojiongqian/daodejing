# 儿童道德经第十章：其二 柔软的人，手不会乱抓

## 第1页

相传，张三丰创太极拳，讲究以柔克刚。

这个传说，不必当成每个细节都查得清的历史。适合孩子记住的，是那幅画面：慢慢转身，手臂像水流，脚步却很稳。

## 第2页

后人讲太极，常画一个壮汉猛冲过来，一拳打得很重。老人侧身、转腰、轻轻一引，壮汉自己的力气找不到落脚处，往前扑空。

这里的柔，带着力量。硬碰硬，是骨头撞骨头；柔，先让对方的力气有地方过去，再找到合适的一点。

## 第3页

太极的慢，让气息有地方走；柔，让身体别拧成一根硬木棍。木棍一折就断，水却能绕过去。

所以“专气致柔，能婴儿乎”，是在问：你能不能不被急气推着跑？能不能有力，却不硬顶？

## 第4页

佛家的天台宗里，智者大师教人从呼吸下手，有“数息”“随息”的方法。

一开始，人可以数自己的呼吸：一、二、三。心跑了，就知道它跑了，再轻轻回来。

## 第5页

有人会把数息数成比赛：我今天数到一千，我比昨天厉害。数到一千，也不等于心更安稳，呼吸反而成了新的骄傲。

数息真正的用处，是给乱跑的心一条小路。心回到呼吸里，身体松下来，眼睛和手也不那么急。

## 第6页

孟子也讲养气。他说，一个人心里如果真的踏实饱满，脸上、身上和手脚上都会自然露出来。

心里有根，人就不会一味摆样子给别人看，身上也少一点飘。

## 第7页

老子讲得更简：“能不能抱一？能不能致柔？能不能像婴儿那样，不把自己拧成一团？”

婴儿饿了会哭，饱了会安，困了会睡。大人却常常一边生气，一边装没事；一边害怕，一边撑得很硬。

## 第8页

第十章最后说“生而不有，为而不恃，长而不宰”。

这句话看起来很大，其实也能落在一件很小的事上：教人骑车。

## 第9页

阿林教小石头骑车。他扶着车后座，嘴里不停：“看前面！别歪！脚用力！手不要抖！”

小石头越听越紧，肩膀耸起来，车头像喝醉了一样晃。阿林怕他摔，又把车抓得更紧。车不倒了，推着它往前走的却仍是阿林那只手。

## 第10页

老人说：“扶可以，别把他的手也扶成你的。”

阿林闭上嘴，只在车快倒时轻轻托一下。小石头喘着气，摔了两次，膝盖沾了土，却慢慢找到自己的平衡。

## 第11页

第三次，车真的往前去了。

阿林跟着跑了几步，手还悬在车后。要不要再抓住？他的手指在车后动了一下，又停住。

## 第12页

就在那一刻，小石头自己踩住了踏板。

阿林把手放下。小石头回头喊：“我会了！”风把他的声音吹得很亮。

## 第13页

阿林心里有一点舍不得，又有一点高兴。

如果他一直扶着，小石头就永远学不会自己骑。教会一个人，也包括让他离开你的手。

## 第14页

太极的柔、数息的慢、孟子的养气、小石头学骑车，都碰到第十章的一条线：身心不散，气息不硬，帮助不变成控制。

“天门开阖，能为雌乎”，可以看门的开合、呼吸的进出：开时能接，合时能静。“明白四达，能无为乎”，是看得明白，也能稳住手，让别人长出自己的能力。

## 第15页

“为而不恃”，是做了，不拿来压人；“长而不宰”，是扶过，不一直抓着。

“玄德”深，因为它常不站在亮处，像扶车时那只及时松开的手。柔软的人手里有力量：稳住自己，在该放手时放手。

# Chapter 010-2: A Soft Person Does Not Grab Wildly

## Page 1

Tradition says that Zhang Sanfeng created tai chi, a practice that uses softness to meet hardness.

We need not read this legend as history in which every detail can be checked. What children can remember is the picture: a person turning slowly, arms like flowing water, feet steady on the ground.

## Page 2

When later people explain tai chi, they often picture a strong man rushing in with a heavy punch. The old practitioner turns aside, turns his waist, and lightly leads the force. The strong man's own strength finds no place to land, and he stumbles forward.

This softness still has strength. Hard against hard is bone striking bone. Softness lets the other force pass, then finds the fitting point.

## Page 3

Tai chi's slowness gives the breath room to move. Its softness keeps the body from twisting into a hard stick. A stick can snap; water can curve around.

So "giving undivided attention to the breath and becoming pliant as a baby" asks: can you stop being pushed around by rushed breath? Can you have strength without hard collision?

## Page 4

In the Tiantai school of Buddhism, Master Zhiyi taught people to begin with breathing, using methods such as counting the breath and following the breath.

At first, one may count: one, two, three. When the mind runs away, one sees that it has run, and gently returns.

## Page 5

Some people turn counting the breath into a contest: today I counted to a thousand, so I am better than yesterday. A thousand breaths do not guarantee a calm heart, and breathing can become a new source of pride.

The real use of counting is to give the wandering heart a small path home. When the heart returns to the breath, the body loosens, and the eyes and hands grow less hurried.

## Page 6

Mencius also spoke of nourishing inner energy. He said that when a person is truly full and steady inside, it shows naturally in the face, body, hands, and feet.

When the heart has roots, there is less need to pose for others, and the body drifts less.

## Page 7

Laozi speaks more simply: can one hold to oneness, become pliant, and be like a baby instead of twisting oneself into a knot?

A baby cries when hungry, rests when full, and sleeps when sleepy. Grown people often feel angry while pretending nothing is wrong, or feel afraid while holding themselves stiff.

## Page 8

At the end, Chapter Ten says: produce and nourish, yet do not claim, boast, or control.

This large sentence can fit inside a small matter: teaching someone to ride a bicycle.

## Page 9

Lin taught Little Stone to ride. He held the back of the seat and kept giving orders: "Look ahead! Don't tilt! Push harder! Don't shake your hands!"

The more Little Stone heard, the tighter he became. His shoulders rose, and the front wheel wobbled as if it were dizzy. Afraid that he would fall, Lin gripped the bicycle even tighter. It stayed upright, but Lin's hand was still doing the work.

## Page 10

The old man said, "You may support him, but do not turn his hands into yours."

Lin closed his mouth. He only steadied the bicycle lightly when it was about to fall. Little Stone breathed hard, fell twice, got dirt on his knees, and slowly found his own balance.

## Page 11

The third time, the bicycle truly moved forward.

Lin ran behind it, his hand still hanging near the seat. Should he grab it again? His fingers curled behind the seat, then stopped.

## Page 12

At that moment, Little Stone caught the pedals by himself.

Lin lowered his hand. Little Stone looked back and shouted, "I can do it!" The wind carried his voice brightly.

## Page 13

Lin felt a little reluctant, and also glad.

If he kept holding on, Little Stone would never learn to ride by himself. Teaching someone also means letting that person leave your hand.

## Page 14

Tai chi's softness, counting breath, Mencius's nourishing of inner energy, and Little Stone's bicycle lesson all touch one line in Chapter Ten: body and heart stay together, breath stays soft, and help does not become control.

"In the opening and shutting of the gates of heaven, can one be like the female?" can be seen in a door or the breath: open to receive, close to settle. "While intelligence reaches everywhere, can one appear without knowledge?" asks us to see clearly, steady our hands, and let others grow their own ability.

## Page 15

"Doing without boasting" means acting without using the action to press others down. "Presiding without controlling" means supporting without always holding on.

"The mysterious Quality" often stays out of the spotlight, like the hand that lets go at the right time. A gentle person can hold great strength: steady yourself, then let go when it is time.
