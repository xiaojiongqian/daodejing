# 儿童道德经第九章：其一 满了，就端不稳

## 第1页

持而盈之，不如其已；
揣而棁之，不可长保。

金玉满堂，莫之能守；
富贵而骄，自遗其咎。

功遂身退，天之道。

## 第2页

第九章先说一个动作：端杯子。杯子八分满，可以稳稳端走；满到边上，还硬要端，每一步都怕洒。

这时，杯子开始管住你：手僵着，脚慢着，眼睛只盯杯沿，别的事都顾不上。

## 第3页

“揣而棁之”，像把笔尖削得太尖。

尖到不能再尖，看起来厉害，可是太细太薄，经不起力气。刚碰到纸，可能就断。

## 第4页

金玉满堂，看起来很富，可满屋宝物也带来满屋担心。

富贵本身是好事。危险在于有了东西、有了位置，就骄傲起来，以为自己永远不会失去。

## 第5页

“功遂身退”，讲的是功成之后的那一步。

事情已经成了，名声也已经够了，就别再抓着功劳不放。太阳过了正午会偏西，月亮圆了也会慢慢缺，这是天的节奏。

## 第6页

“持而盈之”是端着太满的东西，指贪多、不肯停。“已”是停止，知道够了，不再多要。

“棁”是削尖、弄锋利，太锋利反而容易断。“金玉满堂”指财富很多。“咎”是过失及其麻烦。

“功遂身退”是事情完成后让出位置，不死抱功劳。

## 第7页

空空村晚上要点灯读书，还请了邻村的老人来讲故事。

阿林被分到一件大事：准备灯盏、笔和小书桌。他心里亮了一下，像自己也被点成了一盏灯。

## 第8页

“今晚一定要最好。”阿林想。

他把第一盏灯倒得满满的，油鼓到边上，像一层薄薄的月光。小石头提醒：“是不是太满了？”

## 第9页

“多一点，灯就亮一点。”阿林说。

他刚把灯盏端起，手腕一抖，油沿着边滴下来。为了不让油落到书上，他整个人僵住，连呼吸都不敢了。

## 第10页

老人递来一块布，没有责备。

他说：“灯需要油，也需要一点空。太满以后，灯还没亮，它就先把你牵住了。”

## 第11页

阿林有点不服，又去削铅笔。他把每支笔都削得尖尖的，尖到能在灯下闪光。第一笔刚落纸，笔尖就“咔”地断了。

第二支也断了，第三支划破了纸。小石头小声说：“你的笔太厉害，厉害到写不了字。”

## 第12页

读书会开始前，阿林又把书一本本叠得很高，想让大家一眼看见他准备得多。

可是最上面的几本滑下来，纸页散了一地。孩子们赶来捡，故事老人只好等在门口。

## 第13页

阿林终于停住了。

他把灯油倒出一点，把铅笔削得短而稳，把书分成几小摞。东西少了一点，桌面反而能用；光不满到边，灯反而稳稳亮起来。

## 第14页

夜里，故事讲到一半，邻村老人说：“今晚准备得细。”阿林差点站起来说“是我”，却看见大家的脸都在灯下亮着，谁也没有盯着他。

他看看灯，又看看听故事的人，觉得这样正好。灯亮着，故事也顺了；再把名字倒进去，杯子又要满出来。

## 第15页

第九章讲的是：满了，就要停一停。努力到合适处，要知道停；锋利到可用处，不必再削；事情成了，就别把自己钉在功劳上。

把灯点亮，让灯照大家；把事做好，让事情自己往前走。停下，也是往前走。

# Chapter 009-1: When It Is Full, It Cannot Be Carried Steadily

## Page 1

It is better to leave a vessel unfilled, than to attempt to carry it when it is full.  If you keep feeling a point that has been sharpened, the point cannot long preserve its sharpness.

When gold and jade fill the hall, their possessor cannot keep them safe.  When wealth and honours lead to arrogancy, this brings its evil on itself.  When the work is done, and one's name is becoming distinguished, to withdraw into obscurity is the way of Heaven.

## Page 2

Chapter Nine begins with a simple action: carrying a cup. A cup about four-fifths full can be carried steadily. Fill it to the brim, and you worry about spilling with every step.

Now the cup rules you. Your hand stiffens, your feet slow, and your eyes stay on the rim. Everything else is forgotten.

## Page 3

"A point that has been sharpened" is like a pencil shaved too fine.

At its sharpest it looks powerful, but it is too thin to bear any force. The moment it touches the paper, it may break.

## Page 4

Gold and jade filling a hall look rich, but a house full of treasure also brings a house full of worry.

Wealth and honor are good in themselves. The danger begins when having things or position makes a person proud, as if loss could never come.

## Page 5

"Withdraw when the work is done" is about the moment after success.

When the work is done and your name is known, do not cling to the credit. The sun moves west after noon; the full moon slowly wanes. This is the rhythm of heaven.

## Page 6

The word “vessel” means a container, such as a cup or bowl. “Unfilled” means not filled to the top, so some space remains. A “point” is the sharp tip of something.

“Gold and jade” refers to precious things and means wealth here. “Arrogancy” is pride that looks down on others and forgets danger. “Withdraw into obscurity” means stepping back from fame and attention after the work is done.

## Page 7

Kongkong Village would read by lamplight that night, and an elder from the next village was coming to tell stories.

Lin was given an important task: prepare the lamps, pencils, and small reading tables. His heart lit up, as if he too had become a lamp.

## Page 8

"Tonight must be the best," Lin thought.

He filled the first lamp to the very brim. The oil swelled at the edge like a thin layer of moonlight. Little Stone warned him, "Isn't that too full?"

## Page 9

"A little more oil makes a brighter lamp," Lin said.

As soon as he lifted the lamp, his wrist shook and oil dripped down the side. To keep the oil from falling on the books, he froze, and did not even dare to breathe.

## Page 10

The old man handed him a cloth and did not scold.

"A lamp needs oil," he said, "and it also needs a little space. When it is too full, the lamp is not even lit yet, and it is already pulling you along."

## Page 11

Lin was not quite convinced, so he sharpened the pencils. He made every point so fine that it flashed under the lamplight. The first stroke touched the paper, and the point snapped.

The second point snapped too. The third tore the paper. Little Stone whispered, "Your pencils are so powerful they cannot write."

## Page 12

Before the reading began, Lin stacked the books high, wanting everyone to see how much he had prepared.

But the top books slid down, and loose pages scattered across the floor. The children rushed to pick them up, while the visiting storyteller waited at the doorway.

## Page 13

At last Lin stopped.

He poured out a little oil, sharpened the pencils shorter and steadier, and divided the books into small stacks. There was a little less of everything, yet the tables became usable; the lamps no longer brimmed, and their flames stood steady.

## Page 14

At night, halfway through the story, the visiting elder said, "Everything was prepared with such care tonight." Lin nearly stood up to say, "It was me," but he saw everyone's faces shining under the lamps. No one was looking at him.

He looked from the lamps to the listeners and felt that this was just right. The lamps were lit, and the story was moving. If he poured his own name into it, the cup would overflow again.

## Page 15

Chapter Nine is about knowing when to stop. When effort reaches the fitting point, stop; when a point is sharp enough to use, stop sharpening; when the work is done, do not cling to the credit.

Light the lamp and let it shine on everyone. Finish the work and let it go on. Stopping is also moving forward.
