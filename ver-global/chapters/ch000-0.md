# 第000-0章：序言——在名字之前，先看一眼

## 进入

### 第1页

你也许早已在别处遇见老子。

“道可道，非常道。”“上善若水。”“千里之行，始于足下。”这些句子早已离开古书，进入演讲、书法、电影、管理课堂和社交媒体。许多人记得它们的声音，也容易让熟悉感遮住句子原有的问题。

这本书想做的第一件事很简单：在熟悉的句子前停一下，再看一眼。

### 第2页

两千多年以前，《道德经》已经在追问一些至今没有过时的问题：为什么拥有越多，反而越难满足？为什么越想控制，事情越容易失去生机？为什么强者必须学习柔弱，成功者必须知道何时停止，真正的照料又为什么不能以占有为终点？

老子很少替这些问题写下整齐的答案。他更常改变我们提问的位置：从高处移到低处，从满处移到空处，从争先者移到退后的人，从事物已经获得的名字，移到它尚未被名字说尽的部分。

## 老子其人与《道德经》

### 第3页

传统叙事中的老子，姓李名耳，又被称作老聃，曾管理周王室的典籍。司马迁记下的故事说，他见周室衰微，向西而去；行至关口，守关人尹喜请他留下思想。老子写成上下两篇、五千余言，交付书稿，便走出关口，从此失去行踪。后来的想象又为这次西行添上了一头青牛。

这幅画面——一位老人、一头青牛、一道关口和一部薄书——影响了两千年的想象。它之所以动人，不只是因为人物神秘，也因为它把思想写成了一次离开：人在秩序崩坏之际，把最重要的话留在门槛上。

### 第4页

但历史不会像传说那样整齐。司马迁同时保存了几种关于老子身份与年代的说法，今天的研究者也无法把《道德经》的每一句都确定地交还给同一个作者、同一时刻。

“老子”既可能指一位古代思想者，也可能是一个逐渐凝聚的作者形象；《道德经》则可能经过讲述、传抄、选择与编排，才成为后人所见的经典。承认这些不确定，并不会削弱这本书。相反，它让我们看见：一部经典不只诞生于某个人落笔的一夜，也诞生于许多双手长久而谨慎的传递。

### 第5页

今天通行的《道德经》通常分为八十一章：前部分称《道经》，后部分称《德经》，全文不过五千余字。可马王堆帛书把《德经》置于《道经》之前，郭店楚简只保存了部分篇章；不同抄本、注本之间，也留有字词、句读和章序的差异。

因此，我们面对一条流传至今的河。王弼、河上公以及历代读者各自留下解释；新出土的简帛又呈现更早的文本。版本持续变化，核心问题反复回来。

### 第6页

《道德经》篇幅很短，阅读却需要停留。诗的节奏、治理的尺度、修身的工夫与哲学的追问在其中彼此交错，每个概念都保留继续展开的余地。

它由格言、诘问、悖论和反复出现的意象构成：水、谷、婴儿、门、车轮、空器、未经雕琢的木。读者不能只问“这句话是什么意思”，还要问：它在反对怎样的用力？保护怎样的生命？又在什么情形下，可能被误读甚至滥用？

## 道家思想简引

### 第7页

“道”原本可以指道路、方法与行走的方向。在老子笔下，它又指向万物得以生成、变化和返回的根本过程。它先于发号施令的神，也越过任何一条能够写完的公式；每个名字都帮助我们接近它，也各自停在经验的边界上。

所以《道德经》从语言的限度开始，并要求说话的人保持谦逊：地图把方向交给行路者，大地仍在脚下变化；理论解释世界，世界也继续提供修正理论的事实。

### 第8页

“德”也不只是日常意义上的道德品行。它更接近一种内在的力量或成全万物的能力：道怎样在一株草、一条河、一个人或一种治理中具体显现。

与它相连的“自然”，意为“自己如此”：让事物依其条件成为它自己。农人松土，医者治疗，制度阻止伤害；这些行动都要顺着对象的条件展开。

### 第9页

“无为”是最容易被误解的词之一。它不是躺下不做，也不是面对不义保持沉默；它所反对的是多余、强迫、逆势而行的作为。

水会流动，柔弱会回应，空处会容纳。好的行动保持坚定，也保留调整的余地：该开始时开始，该制止时制止，事情完成以后，再把位置交给别人和时间。“无为而无不为”让行动摆脱控制欲的消耗。

### 第10页

老子反复把相反之物放在一起：有与无、难与易、长与短、高与下、强与弱、进与退。每一方都在关系中显现。杯壁提供形状，杯中的空处使它可用；河岸限制水流，也让水有了方向。

后世常以“老庄”并称老子与庄子，他们的思想影响了中国的政治想象、文学艺术、身体修养和宗教传统。哲学意义上的道家与后来形成的道教不能简单画成同一件事，也不能说彼此毫无关系；它们在漫长历史中继承、改写，也彼此争论。

## 这套书怎样打开经典

### 第11页

这套 Global Version 不是把八十一章压缩成八十一条人生金句，也不是给古老词语罩上一层模糊的东方神秘感。每章仍从可核查的原文出发，辨认关键字、版本与古注，再把思想放回能够看见、听见和触摸的世界。

我们不把某一种注解宣布为最后答案。王弼对“无”与生成的思考、河上公对修身与治理的关切、简帛留下的异文，以及现代研究提出的问题，会在需要时并置。差异不是阅读的障碍；它让经典保持诚实的深度。

### 第12页

书中的故事也不只来自一个时代。你会在帛书残片旁看见考古工作者辨认墨迹，在《庄子》的轮匠身上体会语言不能替代的手感；也会遇见西施与混沌、曹参与汉初废墟、屠羊说与范蠡，以及在胜利后交还军权的华盛顿。

柏拉图、康德、道元、约翰·凯奇、纪伯伦，乃至开放系统、博弈论、平台经济和全球贸易，也会进入对话。相似处帮助读者进入问题，差异则标明老子思想的边界。

### 第13页

抽象思想在这里总要找到一件可以承载它的东西。

第一章把“名”放进一张尚未画完的海岸地图；第六章让“谷神”进入一间终于合上电脑、可以听见家人说话的屋子；第八章把“不争”放进共用冷库发生故障的市场；第十章则让“生而不有”走进一座捐赠者必须学会放手的图书馆。

门、路、水、桥、器物和留白不是哲理旁边的装饰。图像承担思想的一部分：它让读者在理解之前先看见，在得到结论之后仍能继续停留。

### 第14页

本系列的文字保持清澈，却不把复杂问题儿童化；它允许诗意，也要求比喻经得起追问。古代章句会进入现代英语，但不会被改装成维多利亚式古语或轻巧的心灵鸡汤；跨文化案例会搭桥，却不会把“道”翻译成另一个传统早已准备好的概念。

所谓“全球版”，让缺少中文或中国思想史背景的读者在准确语境中走近经典，也让熟悉《道德经》的人重新审视那些被背诵遮住的字。

### 第15页

读者将从名字与真实的距离出发，看帛书怎样改变我们对“恒常”的想象；继而进入美丑、善恶、有无相生的关系，辨认“无为”与懒惰、“无知”与愚民术之间的界线。

一张地图会被潮水校正，一个标准会在模仿中变形，一个帝国会在过度干预中耗损。到了“道冲而用之”与“天地不仁”，空与公正也不再是抽象名词：我们必须追问，不偏私是否等于冷酷，留有余地又如何成为真正的作用。

### 第16页

你的视野将从个人呼吸扩展到家庭、组织、市场与国家。谷的空处提醒人不要耗尽注意力；天地之长让我们思考一个系统如何活得比创立者更久；水流进竞争与共同规则之间；“功遂身退”检验成功者能否离开中央；“生而不有”则追问，爱是否允许所爱之人拥有自己的明天。

古老句子由此进入日历被会议填满的办公室、依赖公共河床的经济、需要交接权力的制度，以及父母与孩子之间最难放开的那只手。经典不再只是被解释，也开始反过来解释我们的生活。

## 开卷

### 第17页

你不必先相信老子，也不必把每一章读成同一个答案。可以赞同，可以怀疑，可以在某一句前停留，也可以带着现实经验回来反驳它。

只请暂时保留两种目光：一种看名字、边界、用途与事实，另一种看名字尚未说尽的变化。带着它们，你将遇见一些看似矛盾的邀请——行动而不强迫，创造而不占有，成功而不滞留，知道却仍给未知留下位置。

门已经在这里。它不保证把你带到唯一的终点，只邀请你在跨过去以前，先看一眼门内的幽深，也看一眼自己脚下的路。

# Chapter 000-0: Preface—Before Naming, Look Again

## Entering

### Page 1

You may have met Laozi long before opening this book.

“The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao.” “The highest good is like water.” “A journey of a thousand miles begins beneath one’s feet.” These lines have left the ancient text and entered speeches, calligraphy, films, management classes, and social media. Many people remember their sound, while familiarity can cover the questions they first raised.

This book begins with a simple proposal: pause before the familiar words, and look again.

### Page 2

More than two thousand years ago, the *Dao De Jing* was already asking questions that have not grown old. Why can greater possession make contentment harder? Why does tighter control so often drain life from what we manage? Why must strength learn from softness, why must success know when to stop, and why can genuine care not end in possession?

Laozi rarely arranges neat answers beneath such questions. More often, he changes the place from which we ask: from the high to the low, from fullness to emptiness, from the one who rushes ahead to the one who steps back, and from the name a thing has acquired to everything in it that the name has not exhausted.

## Laozi and the *Dao De Jing*

### Page 3

In the traditional account, Laozi was Li Er, also known as Lao Dan, a keeper of records at the Zhou court. A story preserved by Sima Qian says that, seeing the Zhou order in decline, he traveled west. At the frontier pass, the guardian Yin Xi asked him to leave his teaching behind. Laozi wrote a work in two parts and a little over five thousand characters, delivered the manuscript, and passed beyond the gate. His trail then disappears from history. Later imagination added a dark ox to the journey.

This image—an old man, an ox, a frontier gate, and a slender book—has shaped the imagination for two millennia. Its power lies not only in mystery. It presents thought as a departure: when an order is failing, a traveler leaves his most necessary words at the threshold.

### Page 4

History is less orderly than legend. Sima Qian preserved several accounts of Laozi’s identity and date, and modern scholarship cannot confidently return every line of the *Dao De Jing* to one author at one moment.

“Laozi” may name an ancient thinker, but it may also name an authorial figure who gradually took shape. The text itself may have passed through speech, transmission, selection, and arrangement before becoming the classic later readers inherited. Such uncertainty does not diminish the book. It reminds us that a classic is born not only in a single night of writing, but also through many hands carrying it, carefully and imperfectly, across time.

### Page 5

The received *Dao De Jing* is commonly arranged in eighty-one chapters. Its first section is called the *Dao Jing*, and its second the *De Jing*; together they comprise only a little over five thousand Chinese characters. Yet the Mawangdui silk manuscripts place the *De Jing* before the *Dao Jing*, while the Guodian bamboo slips preserve only portions of the text. Manuscripts and commentarial traditions also differ in wording, punctuation, and sequence.

What reaches us, then, is not an inscription that has never moved, but a river transmitted through time. Wang Bi, Heshang Gong, and generations of readers opened different paths to its banks; excavated silk and bamboo have revealed older stretches of the riverbed. Versions change, while the central questions keep returning.

### Page 6

The *Dao De Jing* is brief and asks for a slow reading. Poetic rhythm, political measure, personal cultivation, and philosophical inquiry cross through its pages, while each concept retains room to unfold.

It is made of sayings, questions, paradoxes, and recurring images: water, valleys, infants, gates, wheels, empty vessels, and uncarved wood. A reader must ask not only, “What does this line mean?” but also: What kind of effort does it resist? What kind of life does it protect? Under what conditions might it be misunderstood—or misused?

## A Brief Entrance into Daoist Thought

### Page 7

The word *dao* can mean a road, a method, or a direction of travel. In Laozi, it also points toward the fundamental process through which things arise, change, and return. It precedes a commanding deity and exceeds every completed formula. Each name helps us approach it and comes to rest at the boundary of experience.

This is why the *Dao De Jing* begins with the limits of language and asks the speaker for humility. A map gives direction to a traveler, while the land continues changing underfoot. A theory explains the world, and the world continues supplying facts that revise it.

### Page 8

*De* means more than moral virtue in the everyday sense. It is closer to an inward potency, or the capacity through which the Dao becomes present in a plant, a river, a person, or a form of government.

The related idea of *ziran*, often translated as naturalness, does not simply mean scenery or wilderness. Literally, it suggests being “so of itself”—allowing something to become what it is through its own conditions. This is not neglect. Farmers still loosen soil, doctors still treat illness, and institutions must still prevent harm. The question is whether help follows the grain of life, or reshapes its object merely to prove the helper’s power.

### Page 9

*Wuwei*, or non-action, is among the most frequently misunderstood Daoist terms. It does not mean lying down and doing nothing, nor does it demand silence in the face of injustice. What it resists is action that is excessive, coercive, or contrary to the conditions before us.

Water moves, softness responds, and empty space receives. Good action can likewise be firm without becoming rigid, timely without becoming excessive: beginning when it is time to begin, intervening when harm must be stopped, and returning one’s place to others and to time when the work is done. “Non-action, yet nothing left undone” is not an escape from action. It is action no longer exhausted by the need to control.

### Page 10

Laozi repeatedly places opposites together: being and non-being, difficult and easy, long and short, high and low, strong and weak, advance and retreat. They are not enemies that must destroy one another; each becomes visible through relation. The wall gives a cup its shape, while the emptiness within makes it useful. Riverbanks limit the water and also give it direction.

Later readers often joined Laozi and Zhuangzi under the name “Lao-Zhuang.” Their thought influenced Chinese political imagination, literature and art, bodily cultivation, and religious traditions. Philosophical Daoism and the organized Daoist religions that developed later should not be treated as identical, but neither are they unrelated. Across a long history, they inherited, transformed, and argued with one another.

## How This Series Opens the Classic

### Page 11

This Global Version does not compress eighty-one chapters into eighty-one slogans for better living, nor does it cover ancient words in a haze of generalized Eastern mystery. Each chapter begins from a verifiable textual ground, considers key terms, variants, and early commentaries, and then returns the thought to a world that can be seen, heard, and touched.

No single commentary is declared the final answer. Wang Bi’s reflections on non-being and generation, Heshang Gong’s concern with self-cultivation and government, variant readings preserved on silk and bamboo, and questions raised by modern scholarship appear when they clarify the text. Difference is not an obstacle to reading. It is part of the classic’s honest depth.

### Page 12

The stories in this series do not belong to one time or place. Beside a fragment of silk, you may watch archaeologists distinguish one faded stroke from another; in the wheelwright of the *Zhuangzi*, you may feel the bodily knowledge that language cannot replace. You will also meet Xishi and Hundun, Cao Can amid the ruins of the early Han, the butcher Yue and Fan Li, and George Washington returning military power after victory.

Plato, Kant, Dōgen, John Cage, and Kahlil Gibran enter the conversation, as do open systems, game theory, platform economies, and global trade. Such encounters do not claim that every culture has always said the same thing. Resemblance opens a door; difference helps us see more precisely what Laozi is—and is not—saying.

### Page 13

In this book, an abstract idea must find something capable of carrying its weight.

Chapter One places naming inside a map of an unfinished coast. Chapter Six brings the valley spirit into a room where a computer is finally closed and a family member can be heard. Chapter Eight sets non-contention in a market whose shared cold-storage room has failed. Chapter Ten lets “giving life without possessing” enter a library whose donor must learn to release control.

Gates, roads, water, bridges, vessels, and blank spaces are not decorations placed beside philosophy. The image bears part of the thought: it lets readers see before they understand, and remain after a conclusion has been reached.

### Page 14

The writing aims to be lucid without making difficult questions childish. It welcomes poetry, but asks every metaphor to withstand inquiry. Ancient lines enter modern English without being disguised in pseudo-archaic language or reduced to weightless self-help. Cross-cultural examples build bridges, but do not translate the Dao into a concept already prepared by another tradition.

“Global” does not mean turning a Chinese classic into a Western one. It means allowing readers without Chinese or a background in Chinese intellectual history to approach the work through an accurate context—and allowing those who know the *Dao De Jing* well to rediscover words hidden by familiarity.

### Page 15

Across the first five chapters, readers begin with the distance between names and reality, then watch silk manuscripts alter our picture of what appears “constant.” They enter the mutual arising of beauty and ugliness, good and bad, being and non-being, and distinguish non-action from laziness and freedom from the political manufacture of ignorance.

A map is corrected by the tide; a standard is distorted through imitation; an empire is depleted by excessive intervention. By the time we reach “the Dao is empty, yet its use is inexhaustible” and “Heaven and Earth are not benevolent,” emptiness and impartiality are no longer abstractions. We must ask whether freedom from favoritism is the same as cruelty, and how leaving room can become a real form of action.

### Page 16

From Chapters Six through Ten, the field widens from breath and attention to families, organizations, markets, and states. The emptiness of the valley warns against exhausting the sources of attention. The endurance of Heaven and Earth asks how a system might outlive its founder. Water enters the tension between competition and shared rules. “Step back when the work is done” tests whether the successful can leave the center. “Give life without possessing” asks whether love can allow what it loves to have a future of its own.

Ancient lines thus enter an office calendar crowded with meetings, an economy dependent on a common riverbed, institutions that must transfer power, and the hand a parent finds hardest to release. The classic is no longer merely interpreted. It begins, in return, to interpret our lives.

## Opening the Book

### Page 17

You do not have to believe Laozi before you begin, nor must you make every chapter yield the same answer. You may agree, doubt, pause before a line, or return with the evidence of your own life and argue with it.

Only keep, for a while, two ways of seeing: one attentive to names, boundaries, uses, and facts; the other attentive to the change no name has exhausted. With both, you will encounter invitations that sound like contradictions—to act without forcing, create without possessing, succeed without remaining at the center, and know while leaving room for the unknown.

The gate is here. It promises no single destination. Before you cross, it asks you to look once into the depth beyond the threshold, and once at the road beneath your feet.
