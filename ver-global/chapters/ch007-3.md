# 第007-3章：公与私之间，谁成全了谁？

## 原文

### 第1页

天长地久。天地所以能长且久者，以其不自生，故能长生。

是以圣人后其身而身先，外其身而身存。

非以其无私邪？故能成其私。

——《道德经》第七章《韬光》

### 第2页

知常容，容乃公，公乃王，王乃天，天乃道，道乃久。

圣人不积，既以为人己愈有，既以与人己愈多。

——《道德经》第十六章、第八十一章

老子把“公”与“私”放进共同生活之中。他追问一个贴近日常的问题：人与他人相处时，怎样尊重对方的目的，也保全自己的位置？

## 释义

### 第3页

第七章虽未出现“公”字，却先说“无私”，继而说“成其私”。这两句之间存在一段漫长因果，付出与所得也超出即时交换。

“私”在这里至少有两种面目：一种把周围的人和事都用于保护自己；另一种则是每个具体生命都需要安顿和完成的自身。前一种私若无限扩张，便会耗损关系，使后一种私失去长久安顿的条件。

### 第4页

“私”还可以从三个层面理解。第一是“身”：安全、名声、位置和寿命。第二是“偏”：只从自己的角度分配代价，让别人承担自己不愿承担的部分。第三是不可替代的个人生活，包括身体、亲情、志趣与选择。

老子警惕前两层成为唯一尺度，同时保留第三层。圣人“外其身”，身体仍受照料；圣人“无私”，自身也得到成全。自我继续存在，并从与他人的良好关系中获得支持。

### 第5页

因此，“成其私”改变了个人所得形成的路径。一个人把安全置于每次决定之上，周围的人便容易替他承担风险；他把声望置于合作之上，同伴也会被安排成观众。自保层层加厚，孤立随之形成。

另一种自我从关系中生长。它坦然承认需要，也愿意接受照料；个人依赖语言、知识、信任和他人的劳动而生活，也以自己的劳动回应这些支持。这样完成的“私”，能够与周围世界一同延续。

### 第6页

那么，“公”是什么？第十六章说：“知常容，容乃公。”这里的“公”让差异共处，也把个人欲望放回共同尺度。

清洁的河流、向所有读者开放的图书馆、依共同尺度运行的规则，都属于共同生活的条件。这些条件由许多人维护，也让每个人得以安顿自己的房间、家庭和愿望。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第7页

**无私：** 王弼解释为“无为于身”，即不围绕自身刻意运作；河上公则强调公正、不偏。两种读法虽着眼点不同，都在限制自我中心的无限扩张。

**成其私：** 个人的安顿与成长从良好关系中产生。它体现间接成全，也要求付出脱离逐笔结算。

**公：** 在老子的语境中，更接近包容、不偏和通达。它容纳个体差异，也限制局部冒充整体。

### 第8页

**互助：** 承认彼此依赖，使帮助在关系中流动，回报也由长期往来形成。

**边界：** 无私与拒绝的权利可以并存。健康的关系允许给予，也允许休息、保留和离开。

**非支配：** 现代政治学用这个词描述不受任意意志摆布的状态。它可以作为理解本章的背景：无论在组织还是公共生活中，占据中心的人都不应把共同事务变成个人意志的延伸。

## 正文

### 第9页

“公”与“私”互相规定。共同语言使个人经验得到表达，基本信任让人把精力用于选择与行动，私人边界则使共同生活保持适当距离。

公保护每个人赖以生活的共同条件；私保护具体的人及其选择。两者各有边界，也彼此支撑。

### 第10页

我们出生时已经置身于许多前人创造的事物之中：语言从上一代传来，道路由陌生人维护，知识经过一代代积累，信任则靠每一次守约慢慢形成。这些共同条件托住个人生活，也让独特的能力和选择获得展开之地。

老子说“圣人不积”，提醒人让流经自己的事物继续流动。知识用于解决问题，关系用于彼此支持，个人所得便会转化成更宽的连接。截留越多，人与世界的通道越容易收窄。

### 第11页

给予也可能暗藏账本：我今天帮助你，是否为了确保你明天偿还？若每次付出都预先规定回报，互助便只是延迟结算的交易。

不计较也与边界共同存在。老子的“无私”首先改变行动的起点：一件事值得去做，便足以成为行动的理由之一。一段关系能否持续，还要看双方是否尊重边界，付出能否流动，疲惫的人能否停下来休息。

### 第12页

把这一章放进组织与公共生活中，可以得到一条有限的提醒：职位服务于职责，共同成果也应当留下参与者的名字。

老子说，最好的统治者功成事遂，百姓却说“我自然”。这句话同样适用于许多协作：协调者排好任务，接住分歧，再把成果交还给参与者。可靠的中心使每个人保留判断和行动的能力。

### 第13页

老子理想中的个体承认需要，也守住边界。他承担责任，让功劳回到共同完成的过程；他接受帮助，也记得回应别人的付出。

这样的人成长得更具体。他知道自己喜欢什么，也让他人的喜好各得其所；他愿意进入关系，也保留独处、休息和改变方向的能力。“成其私”最终成全一个轮廓清楚、愿意与外界相通的自我。

## 回扣

### 第14页

在职场里，个人野心有时会被包装成团队使命，别人的时间便被当作可以无限调用的资源。在家庭里，关心也可能越过边界：父母把控制称作爱，把孩子的人生当作自己愿望的延续。

第七章让我们检视代价的流向：谁得到名声，谁承担疲惫？谁确定共同目标，谁可以表达不同意见？“无私”首先约束拥有更多决定权的人，使承担与权力彼此相称。

### 第15页

公与私的危险出现在一方吞没另一方。私若占尽共同条件，关系便会持续索取；共同之名若抹去个人，亲近也会成为负担。

一所房子需要共同的屋顶，也需要可以关上的房门。屋顶为每个房间挡住风雨，房门则给共同生活划出距离。“无私而成其私”由此落到两个动作上：让他人拥有位置，也把自己安顿在关系之中。

# Chapter 007-3: Between the Common and the Private—Who Completes Whom?

## Original Text

### Page 1

Heaven is enduring; Earth is lasting.

Heaven and Earth endure because they do not live for themselves. Therefore, they can go on living.

Thus the sage places himself behind, yet finds himself in front; puts himself outside consideration, yet remains secure.

Is it not because he is without private designs that what is truly his can be fulfilled?

— *Dao De Jing*, Chapter 7, “Concealing the Light”

### Page 2

Knowing constancy, one becomes capacious. Being capacious, one becomes impartial; being impartial, one becomes fit to lead; being fit to lead, one accords with Heaven, Dao, and duration.

The sage does not hoard. The more he does for others, the more he has; the more he gives to others, the more abundant he becomes.

— *Dao De Jing*, Chapters 16 and 81

Laozi places the common and the private within shared life. His question is close to daily experience: how can a person respect another’s purpose while preserving a place of one’s own?

## Interpretation

### Page 3

The word *gong*—the common, public, or impartial—does not appear in Chapter 7. The chapter first says “without private designs” and then “fulfills what is his.” A long causal path lies between the two, extending beyond immediate exchange.

The private has at least two faces here. One uses everything nearby to protect the self. The other is the particular life that each person must inhabit and complete. When the first expands without limit, it wears down relationships and deprives the second of conditions for lasting security.

### Page 4

The private can be understood at three levels. First comes the body: safety, reputation, position, and lifespan. Second comes partiality: assigning costs from one’s own viewpoint so that others carry what one refuses to bear. Third comes an irreplaceable personal life, including body, attachments, interests, and choices.

Laozi warns against making the first two the only measure while preserving the third. The sage puts the body outside consideration, yet the body still receives care. Private designs recede, and the self is fulfilled through sound relations with others.

### Page 5

“Fulfilling what is his” changes the path by which personal goods arise. When safety stands above every decision, other people are made to carry the risk. When reputation stands above every collaboration, colleagues become an audience. Each new layer of defense deepens isolation.

Another kind of self grows through relationship. It acknowledges need and receives care without shame. A life depends on language, knowledge, trust, and the labor of others, and responds through labor of its own. Such a private life can endure alongside the surrounding world.

### Page 6

What, then, is the common? Chapter 16 says, “Being capacious, one becomes impartial.” Here the common does not begin by pressing everyone into one shape. It begins by refusing to let one person’s appetite masquerade as the measure for all.

A clean river, a library open to every reader, and rules governed by a common measure are all conditions of shared life. Many people maintain them, and they allow each person to sustain a room, a family, and hopes of their own.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 7

**Without private designs:** Wang Bi reads this as not acting deliberately on behalf of the body; Heshang Gong emphasizes impartiality. The two readings begin in different places, but both limit the boundless expansion of self-centeredness.

**Fulfilling what is one’s own:** This does not abolish personal benefit. It allows security and growth to arise from sound relationships. It is indirect fulfillment, not an investment disguised as generosity.

**The common or impartial:** In Laozi’s context, *gong* suggests capaciousness, openness, and freedom from partiality. Its opposite is not individual difference, but a part presenting itself as the whole.

### Page 8

**Mutual aid:** This does not require every contribution to receive an equal return. It begins by acknowledging interdependence and allowing help to circulate.

**Boundary:** Selflessness and the right to refuse can coexist. A healthy relationship makes room for giving, rest, privacy, and departure.

**Non-domination:** Modern political thought uses this term for freedom from arbitrary will. It offers limited background for the chapter: in an organization or in public life, whoever occupies the center should not turn a shared undertaking into an extension of personal will.

## Main Text

### Page 9

The common and the private define one another. Shared language lets personal experience be expressed; basic trust releases energy for choice and action; private boundaries keep shared life at a workable distance.

The common protects not an abstract whole, but the conditions in which people can live. The private protects not arbitrary possession, but the right of a concrete person not to be erased by relationship.

### Page 10

We are born among things created before us. Language comes from earlier generations, roads are maintained by strangers, knowledge accumulates over time, and trust forms through each promise kept. These shared conditions support personal life and allow distinctive abilities and choices to unfold.

Laozi says that the sage does not hoard. The line asks us to let what passes through us continue moving. Knowledge can solve problems, relationships can sustain people, and personal gains can become wider connections. Interception narrows the channels between a person and the world.

### Page 11

Giving can conceal a ledger: do I help today only to guarantee repayment tomorrow? If every contribution specifies its return in advance, mutual aid becomes a transaction with delayed settlement.

Freedom from calculation coexists with boundaries. Laozi’s “without private designs” first changes the starting point of action: the worth of the deed becomes one reason to act. A relationship’s endurance still depends on respected boundaries, circulating contribution, and permission for the exhausted to rest.

### Page 12

Placed in organizational and public life, the chapter offers a limited reminder: an office serves its duties, and shared achievements should retain the names of those who contributed.

Laozi says that when the best ruler completes the work, the people say, “We did it naturally, by ourselves.” The line also describes many forms of collaboration. A coordinator assigns tasks, receives disagreement, and returns the achievement to its participants. A reliable center leaves each person with judgment and the capacity to act.

### Page 13

Laozi’s ideal individual acknowledges needs and maintains boundaries. Such a person accepts responsibility and returns credit to the shared process; they receive help and remember to answer another person’s effort.

This person becomes more distinct. They know what they enjoy and let the preferences of others retain their place. They enter relationships while preserving solitude, rest, and the capacity to change direction. The chapter finally fulfills a self with clear contours and open contact with the world.

## Return to the Original

### Page 14

At work, personal ambition can be packaged as the team’s mission, turning other people’s time into a resource available without limit. Within families, care can also cross a boundary: parents may call control love and turn a child’s life into a continuation of their own wishes.

Chapter 7 asks us to trace where costs flow. Who receives the name, and who carries the exhaustion? Who defines the shared goal, and who may voice a different view? “Without private designs” first restrains those with greater power to decide, bringing responsibility into proportion with authority.

### Page 15

Danger appears when either the common or the private swallows the other. Private appetite can consume shared conditions until relationship becomes extraction; the language of the common can erase a person until closeness becomes a burden.

A house needs a shared roof and doors that can close. The roof protects every room from the storm, and each door gives common life a workable distance. “Fulfilling the private through freedom from private designs” comes down to two actions: give others a place, and settle oneself within the relationship.
