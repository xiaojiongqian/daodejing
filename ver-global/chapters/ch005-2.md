# 第005-2章：不仁与刍狗

## 原文

### 第1页

“天地不仁，以万物为刍狗”很容易被读成一句冷酷的话：既然天地不偏爱谁，统治者是否也可以不顾具体人的痛苦？

法家对《老子》的吸收，使这个问题变得实际。相同的“无为”“不仁”，放在老子那里限制统治者的私意，放进君主权术以后，却可以用来要求臣民服从，并让君主更难被揣测。

### 第2页

差别可以先从“无为”看。老子要求主政者少以个人欲望扰动天下；韩非讨论君道时，则强调君主不要轻易显露好恶，让臣下无法预先迎合，再以名责实、按结果考核。

一个是在限制权力任性，一个是在提高权力的控制能力。用字相近，实际约束的对象已经不同。

## 释义

### 第3页

老子用“天地不仁”说明自然依共同条件运行。雨落向田野，寒暑循季节更替，个人的贤愚与声望都无法改变这套节律。人面对苦难时仍要回应；自然的无偏私与人的照料各有所在。

两者一旦混同，“不偏私”便容易成为冷漠的借口。

### 第4页

“圣人不仁”要求统治者把亲疏放在公共尺度之后。亲近的人犯法，规则照常适用；疏远的人申诉，程序也应接住他的理由。治理随个人喜怒改变，今日的恩惠很快便会成为明日的不安。

所以，不仁在这里首先要求去除偏爱。它仍需面对人的处境，也仍要说明规则为何如此、伤害如何补救。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第5页

“刍狗”是古代祭礼使用的草扎狗。仪式开始，执事将它陈设好；仪式结束，又把它撤下。庄子后来的相关叙述更清楚地写出它在仪式前后的不同待遇。这个形象提醒人分清一时尊奉与永久占有。

“橐籥”指古代风箱。人拉开空腔，空气进入；手柄压下，气流便送向炉火。老子借此说明空处也能发生作用。这一形象谈天地如何生发，政治制度仍需另作讨论。

## 正文

### 第6页

法家对“无为”的改写，首先落在君主如何面对臣下。君主收起个人喜好，把事务交给臣下；臣下陈述职责，君主再按结果核验。讨好由此失去直接作用，最后的奖惩权仍集中在君主手中。

这与老子的重点不同。老子要减少主政者的私意干扰；韩非更关心怎样让臣下无法欺骗主君。前者限制统治冲动，后者使统治更有效。

### 第7页

法家谈赏罚时，更关心命令能否执行。临时宽免可能被视为削弱法令，私人同情也可能被视为破坏威慑。商鞅的政治思路尤其强调让人口和资源服从耕战，使民众难以形成不受国家控制的力量。

这里呈现国家有明确目的的组织与驱使，与天地“不偏私”的自然秩序分属两种逻辑。

### 第8页

刍狗与百姓在原句中承担不同位置。老子借祭器说明天地无私；耕战国家的制度则登记和征发百姓，并按他们能够提供的粮食与兵役计算。

韩非说“民之性，恶劳而乐佚”，由此主张以赏罚把人驱向农战。国家由一个人的粮食和兵役计算价值，个人安排自身生活的空间便随之收窄。

### 第9页

《解老》《喻老》显示韩非系统地把老子的若干概念放进君臣关系：君主隐藏意图，占据权位，以规则和考核观察臣下。

读者因此要看清概念落在谁身上。若“无为”要求君主克制自己，它减少权力的扰动；若“无为”只要求君主不露声色，它也可能让权力更难受到监督。

### 第10页

秦朝展示了高度动员的两面。它迅速灭六国、统一度量衡，也征发大量人口承担军役与工程。传统叙事中，陈胜、吴广遇雨误期，担忧依秦法受罚，于是决定起兵。

秦亡牵涉多重原因，陈胜、吴广的故事则展示了一条清楚的因果：大雨阻断行程，误期触发刑罚风险，申诉路径又十分狭窄，普通人的选择遂被压缩到服刑、逃亡或反抗。

## 回扣

### 第11页

历史上确实有把人只按赋税、粮食和兵役计算的治理。老子所谓“不仁”要求公正地对待亲疏，“无为”要求主政者少以私欲扰民。两条要求都把任意处罚和隐秘操控置于批判之下。

### 第12页

区分老子与法家，可以看三件具体的事：权力是否需要说明理由，受罚者是否能够申诉，规则是否也约束制定规则的人。

相同的古字进入不同制度，会产生不同生活。辨认这种差别，比把“不仁”简单解释成仁慈或冷酷更重要。

# Chapter 005-2: Non-Benevolence and Straw Dogs

## Original Text

### Page 1

“Heaven and earth are not benevolent; they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs” can easily sound cruel. If Heaven favors no one, may a ruler also ignore the suffering of particular people?

Legalist uses of Laozi make the question practical. The same words—non-action and non-benevolence—can restrain a ruler’s private will in one setting, yet in the language of statecraft help subjects obey and make the ruler harder to read.

### Page 2

The difference appears first in non-action. Laozi asks those who govern to disturb the world less with private desire. Han Feizi, writing about rulership, advises the sovereign not to reveal preferences too readily, lest ministers learn to flatter them, and to compare assigned duties with actual results.

One account restrains arbitrary power; the other increases power’s capacity for control. Similar language now governs a different person.

## Interpretation

### Page 3

When Laozi says that heaven and earth are not benevolent, he describes an order governed by shared conditions. Rain falls across fields, seasons turn, and personal virtue or reputation cannot alter their rhythm. Human beings still answer suffering with care; nature’s impartiality and human responsibility each retain their place.

Once the two are confused, impartiality can become an excuse for coldness.

### Page 4

The sage’s non-benevolence places closeness behind the public measure. A favorite who breaks the law remains subject to it; a stranger with a valid grievance finds a procedure able to receive her reasons. When administration follows personal affection, today’s favor becomes tomorrow’s insecurity.

Non-benevolence first removes favoritism. It must still attend to human circumstances, explain rules, and provide remedies for harm.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 5

Straw dogs were ritual objects. An attendant arranged them before the rite and removed them when it ended. A related passage in the *Zhuangzi* makes this change of treatment especially clear. The image asks readers to distinguish temporary honor from permanent possession.

A bellows is empty in the middle. A hand draws its chamber open and air enters; the handle presses down and sends air toward the fire. Laozi uses it to show how an empty space can have an effect. The image concerns the generative activity of heaven and earth, while political institutions require a separate inquiry.

## Main Text

### Page 6

The Legalist revision of non-action appears first in the ruler’s conduct toward ministers. The ruler withholds personal preferences and delegates tasks; ministers state their duties, and the ruler tests each claim against its result. Flattery loses some of its direct use, while final reward and punishment remain in the ruler’s hands.

Laozi’s emphasis is different. He seeks to reduce interference caused by the ruler’s private will. Han Feizi is more concerned with preventing ministers from deceiving the sovereign. The former restrains the urge to rule; the latter makes rule more effective.

### Page 7

Legalist discussions of reward and punishment focus on whether commands can be enforced. An exceptional pardon may weaken a statute; private sympathy may weaken deterrence. Shang Yang’s political program places particular weight on directing population and resources toward agriculture and war, while limiting independent sources of social power.

This is not the impartiality of the seasons. It is organization by a state with explicit purposes.

### Page 8

The straw dog and the people occupy different places in Laozi’s sentence. The ritual object illuminates heaven’s lack of favoritism. States organized for agriculture and war, by contrast, registered and levied people according to the grain and military service they could provide.

Han Feizi writes that people dislike labor and enjoy ease, and therefore argues for rewards and punishments that direct them toward farming and war. As the state measures a person through grain and service, the person’s room to shape a life of his own narrows.

### Page 9

Han Feizi’s *Explaining Laozi* and *Illustrating Laozi* place several Daoist concepts systematically within relations between ruler and minister: conceal intention, occupy the position of authority, assign duties, and examine performance.

We therefore need to ask whom a concept restrains. When non-action restrains the ruler, it reduces disturbance by power. When it means only that the ruler remains unreadable, it can also make power harder to supervise.

### Page 10

The Qin dynasty shows two sides of concentrated mobilization. It conquered the six states and standardized weights and measures, while conscripting large populations for military service and construction. In the traditional account, Chen Sheng and Wu Guang were delayed by rain, feared punishment under Qin law, and chose rebellion.

Qin’s fall had many causes. The story of Chen Sheng and Wu Guang still reveals a clear chain: rain blocked the journey, delay triggered the risk of punishment, and a narrow path of appeal reduced ordinary choices to punishment, flight, or revolt.

## Return to the Original

### Page 11

History does contain systems that counted people chiefly by tax, grain, and military service. Laozi’s non-benevolence asks that near and distant be treated without favoritism; non-action asks rulers to disturb people less with private desire. Both place arbitrary punishment and hidden control under criticism.

### Page 12

The difference becomes clearer through three concrete questions: must power give reasons, can the punished appeal, and are rule-makers also bound by rules?

The same ancient word produces different daily lives when placed in different institutions. Recognizing that difference matters more than labeling non-benevolence either compassion or cruelty.
