# 第003-2章：无为与自发秩序

## 原文

### 第1页

1776年，亚当·斯密在《国富论》讨论国内投资时写道，个人追求自身利益，可能“受着一只看不见的手的指导”，促成并非出于本意的社会效果。这句话后来被广泛用来概括分散行动如何产生非预期秩序，但它并不保证每一次逐利都会带来整体福祉。

### 第2页

这与老子“我无事而民自富”的说法形成了一处可供比较的呼应。

斯密对重商主义的批判同样反对政府掌控市场的每一个细节。后来的自由主义传统常以“守夜人”政府概括更狭义的国家职能；这一说法不是斯密本人对其全部政治经济思想的总结。

### 第3页

20世纪，弗里德里希·哈耶克在《通往奴役之路》等著作中警告：试图以中央计划统摄复杂社会，可能损害自由。

他在多部著作中阐发“自发秩序”：语言、道德、市场与普通法等秩序，往往由许多个体行动逐步形成，而非出自一个中央设计者的完整蓝图。

### 第4页

20世纪80年代英美的新自由主义改革减少了若干经济领域的国家干预，让更多资源分配交由市场。其价值基础与政策后果都不能等同于老子的“无为”；可比较之处，仅在于两者都对全面控制抱有疑虑。

### 第5页

进入21世纪，理查德·塞勒与卡斯·桑斯坦推广了“助推”（nudge）策略：通过默认选项和选择架构影响行为，同时保留退出或另选的可能，而非直接命令。

## 释义

### 第6页

本章把老子的“无为”与近代社会科学中的“自发秩序”、市场协调和“助推”放在一起讨论。几种理论各有来路，却在一个问题上相遇：当商人、工人、家庭和地方机构各自掌握一部分情况时，治理者应当管到哪里，又应当在哪里停手？

### 第7页

这些主张各自处理不同的问题。斯密与哈耶克关心个人选择、分散知识和规则的边界；20世纪80年代的改革把更多交易交给市场，也带来了具体的收益与代价；“助推”仍由政策制定者安排默认选项，同时保留另选的权利。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第8页

- **看不见的手**：斯密用以描述个体利益追求如何在无意间带来公共利益的机制。
- **自发秩序（Spontaneous Order）**：哈耶克概念，指复杂社会秩序通过去中心化的个体行动自然演化而成。
- **助推（Nudge）**：行为经济学用语，通过设计默认选项与选择架构微调行为，而避免直接命令式干预。

## 正文

### 第9页

比较这些思想，可以先看一个普通的办事窗口。申请人从第一个窗口领到表格，填完后走到第二个窗口，又抄写一遍相同内容；印章越盖越多，开店的时间也随之推迟。食品安全、工资和申诉仍需要可执行的规则，它们把风险留在经营者一侧，也给顾客和雇员留下追责的路径。

老子说“我无为而民自化”，要求统治者先收住好大喜功的手，省去为显示政绩而制造的新命令，同时继续承担公共责任。

### 第10页

斯密写“看不见的手”时，讨论的是商人如何安排资本。一位商人衡量风险与收益时，可能选择本国交易，并在无意间增加本地的生产与就业。社会效果不是他最初的目的，却可能由他的选择产生。

但商人也可能垄断、欺骗或把成本转给别人。这个例子说明秩序有时不靠统一指令形成，并不证明每一种逐利都值得放任。

### 第11页

哈耶克进一步追问：一个中央办公室如何同时知道各地的收成、库存、运输延误和顾客偏好？面包师知道今天的面粉价格，店主知道哪一排货架先空，顾客知道自己是否愿意换一种商品。这些零散信息很难在同一张报表里及时出现。

他的警告与老子相近之处，在于承认决策者所知有限。命令写得再完整，也可能遗漏现场已经发生的变化。

### 第12页

但差异同样重要。道家的“无为”连着不争、少私与对统治欲的节制；现代自由主义内部则同时讨论个人自由、分散知识、权利边界与效率，不能只归结为自利假设。

### 第13页

“助推”位于放任与命令之间。例如，把加入养老金计划设为默认选项，职员仍可退出，加入者也省去一连串申请。政策保留重新选择的权利，同时改变了最省事的那条路径。

这可以与“少用强制”对照，但仍是一种有意设计，不能直接叫作老子的“无为”。

### 第14页

“放手”也会产生明确的受益者。大型平台、雇主或垄断者拥有更多资金、信息和谈判能力；若监管同时退去，普通消费者和劳动者未必因此得到更多自由。

所以，减少一项规定之前，还要问由谁承担风险，受损者能否申诉，以及竞争者是否真能进入。

### 第15页

“无为”要求统治者节制欲望与争夺；“自发秩序”主要说明分散行动和知识怎样协调。两者都提醒决策者承认资料的边界，并给现场经验留下修正规则的空间。

## 回扣

### 第16页

把“无为”带回当代，可以从具体问题开始：哪些规则防止伤害，哪些手续只在重复证明服从；哪些决定需要统一，哪些应交给更了解现场的人。

### 第17页

治理者很少能一次找到永久答案。规定实施以后，要看小店是否仍能开门，工人是否敢申诉，顾客是否有别的选择；情况变了，规则也应当复查。

老子的提醒落在这一连串实际动作上：删去重复手续，保留安全边界，听取现场反馈，再根据结果修正规则。勤政由问题是否得到解决来衡量。

# Chapter 003-2: Non-Action and Spontaneous Order

## Original Text

### Page 1

In 1776, while discussing domestic investment in *The Wealth of Nations*, Adam Smith wrote that a person pursuing private advantage may be “led by an invisible hand” to promote an end that was no part of the intention. The phrase was later generalized as an account of unintended order, but it does not promise that every pursuit of profit produces the public good.

### Page 2

This offers a useful comparison with Laozi’s claim that when the ruler refrains from interference, the people prosper. Smith’s critique of mercantilism likewise resists state control of every market detail. A later liberal tradition used the image of a “night-watchman” state for a narrower public role; the phrase is not Smith’s own summary of his entire political economy.


### Page 3

In the twentieth century, Friedrich Hayek warned in *The Road to Serfdom* and other works that attempts to govern a complex society through central planning could damage freedom. Across several books he developed the idea of spontaneous order: language, morals, markets, and common law often grow from many decentralized actions rather than from one complete design.


### Page 4

The neoliberal reforms of the 1980s in the United States and United Kingdom reduced state intervention in several economic fields and left more allocation to markets. Their values and consequences should not be equated with Laozi’s non-action. The limited point of comparison is their suspicion of comprehensive control.

### Page 5

In the twenty-first century, Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein popularized “nudges”: changes to defaults and choice architecture that influence behavior while preserving the option to choose otherwise.

## Interpretation


### Page 6

This chapter places Daoist non-action beside modern ideas of spontaneous order, market coordination, and nudging. Each has its own history, yet they meet at a narrower question: when merchants, workers, households, and local institutions each hold part of the relevant knowledge, how far should government reach, and where should it stop?


### Page 7

The positions address different problems. Smith and Hayek attend to individual choice, dispersed knowledge, and the limits of rules. Reforms in the 1980s entrusted more transactions to markets and produced specific gains and costs. Policymakers still design a nudge while preserving the right to choose another option.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 8

- **Invisible hand**: Smith’s metaphor for how self-interested behavior can lead to public benefits.
- **Spontaneous order**: Hayek’s idea that complex social orders arise without central design.
- **Nudge**: Behavioral economics technique of influencing choices subtly via defaults and framing.

## Main Text

### Page 9

Consider an ordinary licensing office. An applicant collects a form at the first window, fills it in, walks to the second, and copies the same information again. As the stamps accumulate, the opening day recedes. Enforceable rules for food safety, wages, and complaints keep risk with the operator and give customers and employees a path to redress.

When Laozi says, “I do nothing, and the people transform themselves,” he does not tell rulers to disappear. He first asks them to restrain the urge to issue new orders merely to display activity.

### Page 10

Smith introduces the invisible hand while discussing how merchants place their capital. In weighing risk and return, a merchant may choose domestic trade and unintentionally support local production and employment. The public effect was not the original aim, yet it can follow from the choice.

The same merchant may also deceive, monopolize, or transfer costs to others. The example shows that order can arise without a single command; it does not show that every pursuit of gain should be left alone.

### Page 11

Hayek asks how a central office could know, at once, local harvests, inventories, shipping delays, and customer preferences. A baker knows today’s flour price, a shopkeeper knows which shelf emptied first, and a customer knows whether another product will do. Such knowledge rarely appears in one report soon enough.

His warning resembles Laozi’s in one respect: decision-makers know less than they are tempted to claim. A complete-looking order can still miss what has already changed on the ground.

### Page 12

Yet differences matter. Daoist non-action is tied to non-contention, fewer private designs, and restraint of the urge to rule. Modern liberal traditions also debate liberty, dispersed knowledge, rights, and efficiency; they cannot be reduced to self-interest alone.

### Page 13

A nudge sits between neglect and command. A workplace pension, for example, may make enrollment the default while allowing every employee to opt out. People retain a choice, but joining no longer requires a chain of applications.

This can be compared with less coercive government, but it remains deliberate design and is not a modern translation of *wu wei*.

### Page 14

Stepping back also produces identifiable beneficiaries. Large platforms, employers, and monopolies possess more money, information, and bargaining power. When oversight retreats, consumers and workers do not automatically become freer.

Before removing a rule, policymakers must ask who will bear the risk, whether the injured can appeal, and whether competitors can actually enter.

### Page 15

Non-action restrains the ruler’s desires and rivalries; spontaneous order chiefly explains coordination among dispersed actions and knowledge. Both ask decision-makers to acknowledge the limits of their data and leave room for local experience to revise a rule.

## Return to the Original

### Page 16

Bringing Laozi into contemporary debate can begin with concrete questions: which rules prevent harm, which procedures merely demand repeated proof of obedience, which decisions require uniformity, and which belong with people who know the local circumstances.

### Page 17

No government finds a permanent answer at the first attempt. After a rule takes effect, officials must see whether a small shop can still open, whether a worker can safely complain, and whether a customer has another choice. When circumstances change, the rule must be examined again.

Laozi’s restraint appears in practical acts: remove repeated procedures, preserve safeguards, listen to local feedback, and revise rules in light of results. Diligence is measured by whether the problem is resolved.
