# 第011-2章：空处属于谁——公共空间与共用规则

## 原文

### 第1页

1961年，简·雅各布斯在《美国大城市的死与生》中观察城市街道。她反对把街道只看成车辆和行人快速通过的管道，也反对相信一片宽阔、整齐的开放空间会自动产生公共生活。

街道之所以安全而有活力，往往因为住宅、店铺、工作场所和公共设施在不同时间带来不同的人。沿街的门、窗与日常目光，使看似普通的人行道成为许多人共同维持的生活场所。

### 第2页

雅各布斯用“街道芭蕾”描写这些自行衔接的日常动作：店主拉开卷帘门，孩子沿人行道走向学校，送货员把推车停在店门旁，邻居从窗边看见货物进入街角。

这个譬喻强调相邻建筑、时间节律与使用者之间形成的关系。铺路石提供地面，沿街活动让人停留，日常照应则把一段通道变成共同生活的场所。

### 第3页

2009年，埃莉诺·奥斯特罗姆因对经济治理、尤其是公地治理的研究获得诺贝尔经济学奖。她长期研究牧场、森林、渔场和灌溉系统，反对把共享资源的命运只写成两个结局：要么交给中央命令，要么切分为私人财产。

她的研究显示，在一定条件下，实际使用资源的人能够形成边界、监督、协商和纠纷处理规则，使共用持续下去。共同使用依靠规则维持，而贴近现场、可由参与者修订的规则更能回应变化。

### 第4页

奥斯特罗姆讨论的“公共池资源”具有较严格的经济学含义：排除使用者较难，而一个人的取用会减少别人可得的份额。渔场和灌溉水是典型例子。

城市广场或人行道不能简单归入同一类别。一个人走过广场，通常不会把广场消耗掉；但当空间拥挤、被长期占用或准入受限时，不同使用之间仍会发生冲突。两者可比较之处，在于共用需要边界、责任与协商，而不是在于它们属于同一种资源。

### 第5页

现代城市中还有一种相反的设计：空间看起来向所有人开放，细节却在暗中筛选谁能停留。

长椅中间加上固定分隔，平整处竖起尖钉，屋檐下撤去可以坐卧的边缘。这类做法常被称为“排斥性设计”或“敌意建筑”。它以秩序、维护或安全为理由，把某些身体移出视野，贫困、无家可归与公共服务不足仍留在原处。

空地仍然在那里，使用它的门却已经变窄。

## 释义

### 第6页

本篇把第十一章与城市公共空间、共享治理和排斥性设计放在一起讨论。几种思想在一个问题上相遇：一处空间即使保持通道，是否已经允许不同的人进入和停留？

老子的车毂、陶器和房室强调有形边界与无形空处共同成就作用。现代城市问题又增加了一层：谁能进入这片空，谁可以停留，谁有权改变规则，以及维护成本由谁承担？

### 第7页

这些现代概念不能倒过来成为第十一章的唯一解释。

雅各布斯关心城市街道怎样从多样日常活动中获得活力；奥斯特罗姆研究共享资源如何形成可持续制度；对排斥性设计的批评则揭示物件和空间怎样执行看不见的禁令。老子没有提出一套城市规划或公地治理理论。

可供对照的是一个基本提醒：物体提供边界，物体之间以及人与空间之间形成的通路共同决定作用。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第8页

- **街道芭蕾与街道上的眼睛**：雅各布斯用来描述不同人群在日常时间中共同形成的街道秩序；不是要求居民互相监视，也不能替代公正的公共安全制度。
- **公共池资源（common-pool resource）**：较难排除使用者、同时一个人的取用可能减少他人份额的资源。城市公共空间只能在冲突、边界和共治问题上与之类比。
- **使用中的规则（rules-in-use）**：人们在实际合作中遵循并持续调整的规则，不只指写在纸上的正式规定。
- **排斥性设计（hostile or exclusionary design）**：通过物体、设施或空间布局阻止特定行为，并常由此排除特定人群的设计做法。

## 正文

### 第9页

一座广场可以在航拍照片中显得极其开阔：石面从建筑前一直铺向远处，视线沿整齐立面展开。

树荫、饮水处、厕所和座位决定人能停留多久。老人走过开阔石面，很快需要寻找休息处；照料者带着孩子转身离开，户外工作者也只能继续赶路。广场承接了穿行，生活却难以在此停留。

几何意义上的空，需要设施与关系把它转化成社会意义上的“用”。

### 第10页

相反，空间也可能被“有”慢慢占尽。

咖啡桌越过店铺边界，广告牌挤进通道，共享单车堵住坡道，临时围栏在活动结束后仍未拆除。每一件东西都有理由，每一种占用都能说明自己的收益；累积到最后，不消费的人只剩一条狭窄的穿行线。

荒废会损伤公共空间，过量占用也会压缩共同使用所需的空处。

### 第11页

雅各布斯的观察提醒我们，街道的活力并不来自把人清走以后留下的整洁。住宅入口、日常店铺、不同时间的活动和愿意停留的人，共同使一段路拥有连续的生命。

但“街道上的眼睛”也必须谨慎理解。熟悉的目光可以带来照应，也可能把陌生人视为可疑者；非正式秩序能够补充制度，却不能成为歧视、围观或私刑的许可。

真正的公共性容纳差异，也让空间具备处理冲突、保护弱者的能力。

### 第12页

奥斯特罗姆的研究则提醒我们，共用依靠规则持续。

若没有任何边界，一次大型活动便可能长期占住广场；若规则只由远离现场的人制定，又可能把摊贩、儿童、轮椅使用者和附近居民的实际经验排除在外。规则需要说明什么行为会伤害共同使用，也要让受影响者有表达、申诉和修订的渠道。

这项理解来自当代城市经验，也能帮助读者看见：“留空”是一项持续的治理工作。

### 第13页

排斥性设计把这一问题翻到另一面。

一张长椅被密集扶手切成短段，疲惫的人只能坐直；屋檐下排着凸起物，身体也只能绕开。设计省去了“你不得停留”的牌子，禁令已经进入金属、角度和间距。

秩序与安全都是真实需要，公共空间也有使用边界。若一座城市只改变椅子，同时省去住所、卫生设施或支持服务，它处理的只是问题的可见度，人的处境仍留在原地。

### 第14页

“空处可用”还取决于使用者的身体。

对步行者足够宽的门，可能容不下轮椅转身；对年轻人只是几级台阶的入口，可能让推车者止步；没有遮荫的空地，在凉爽季节可以聚会，在酷热中却难以使用。声音、光线、文字和费用也会形成看不见的门槛。

所以，可进入不是一项抽象属性。它总要追问：哪一种身体可以进入，能停留多久，需要付出什么代价，又是否能够安全离开。

### 第15页

公共空间需要明确责任，也需要限制个人或团体的长期占据。

好的规则既要保护通道，也要允许停留；既要限制伤害，也要避免把“不合管理者心意”直接等同于危险；既要维护清洁与设备，也要让被规则影响的人参与复查。这里没有一张适用于所有广场的永久图纸。

会议里的一把空椅也是如此。若发言顺序、专业术语和权力差距使新来者始终沉默，椅子虽然空出来，声音却没有入口。空间是否共同，不只看它有没有主人，还要看它是否只能服务最有力量的使用者。

## 回扣

### 第16页

回到第十一章，轮毂的孔不是任意的洞，它要让车轴进入；陶器的腹不是抽象的虚无，它要能够盛受；房屋的空也不只是墙内面积，它通过门窗与人的生活相连。

“无之以为用”因此还可以提醒今天的城市：空处必须真正形成通路。若一扇门只为少数身体而开，一张长椅只允许短暂停靠，一片广场只能在付费时停留，那么它在形式上仍空，在关系上却已被占满。

### 第17页

清晨，清洁人员推车穿过广场，车轮在地面留下湿痕。摊贩沿着湿痕把货架推到街角，撑开遮篷；孩子绕过货架跑向水池，等车的人则把手提袋放到长椅旁。

这些动作依次接入同一片空处，使它在通行、停留、交易、游戏与休息之间改变用途。

公共的空，更接近一种不能被任何单一用途永久封闭的承诺。

墙为它定出边界，门让人进入；规则保护共同使用，而共同使用也必须能够回来修正规则。

空处真正的问题，从来不只是“这里有什么”。

还包括：谁能进来，谁可以留下，谁仍有权使它发生新的作用。

# Chapter 011-2: Who Is the Open Space For?—Public Space and Rules for Shared Use

## Original Text

### Page 1

In 1961, Jane Jacobs examined city streets in *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. She resisted treating streets merely as conduits through which vehicles and pedestrians should move quickly. She also resisted the belief that a broad, orderly open space automatically produces public life.

Streets often become safe and lively because homes, shops, workplaces, and public institutions bring different people at different hours. Doors, windows, and ordinary attention along the street turn an unremarkable sidewalk into a place maintained by many lives.

### Page 2

Jacobs used the image of a “sidewalk ballet” for daily movements that join of their own accord: a shopkeeper raises the shutter, children follow the pavement toward school, a delivery worker parks a cart beside the door, and a neighbour at a window watches goods enter the corner shop.

The image emphasizes relationships among neighbouring buildings, rhythms of time, and the people who use the street. Paving supplies the ground, daily activity gives people reasons to remain, and ordinary attention turns a route into a place of shared life.

### Page 3

In 2009, Elinor Ostrom received the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for her work on economic governance, especially the governance of the commons. Her research on pastures, forests, fisheries, and irrigation systems challenged a simple choice often imposed upon shared resources: either central command or division into private property.

Under certain conditions, she showed, resource users can develop boundaries, monitoring, negotiation, and methods for resolving disputes that allow shared use to continue. Common use depends on rules, and rules closer to local knowledge and open to revision by participants can respond more readily to change.

### Page 4

The “common-pool resources” studied by Ostrom have a specific economic meaning. It is difficult to exclude users, while one person's extraction reduces what remains available to others. Fisheries and irrigation water are standard examples.

A city square or sidewalk does not simply belong to the same category. One person's passage usually does not consume the square. Yet uses may conflict when space becomes crowded, permanently occupied, or restricted. The comparison concerns boundaries, responsibility, and shared governance—not an identity between the resources.

### Page 5

Contemporary cities also contain an opposite kind of design: a space appears open to everyone while its details quietly select who may remain.

Fixed dividers break up benches, spikes cover flat surfaces, and every ledge beneath shelter is removed. Such measures are often called hostile, defensive, or exclusionary design. They may be justified in the language of order, maintenance, or safety, yet they can leave poverty, homelessness, and inadequate public services untouched while moving certain bodies out of sight.

The open ground remains, but the door to its use has narrowed.

## Interpretation

### Page 6

This essay places Chapter Eleven beside public space, commons governance, and exclusionary design. They meet at a narrower question: when a space keeps a passage open, does it also let different people enter and remain?

Laozi's wheel hub, vessel, and room show how visible boundaries and invisible openings create function together. The modern city adds another layer: who may enter the opening, who may stay, who can revise its rules, and who bears the cost of maintaining it?

### Page 7

These modern ideas should not be turned backward into the only interpretation of Chapter Eleven.

Jacobs asks how streets acquire vitality from diverse daily activity. Ostrom studies the institutions through which shared resources can endure. Criticism of exclusionary design reveals how objects and layouts enforce commands that may never be written on a sign. Laozi did not propose a theory of urban planning or commons governance.

The useful comparison is more basic: objects provide boundaries, and paths formed among objects, people, and space also determine function.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 8

- **Sidewalk ballet and eyes on the street:** Jacobs's images for the street order created by varied people across ordinary time. They do not require neighbours to monitor one another constantly, nor can they replace just institutions of public safety.
- **Common-pool resource:** A resource from which users are difficult to exclude and in which one person's use may reduce another's share. Urban public space is analogous only in questions of conflict, boundaries, and shared governance.
- **Rules-in-use:** The rules people actually follow and continually adjust in cooperation, not only the formal rules written on paper.
- **Hostile or exclusionary design:** Objects, facilities, or spatial arrangements intended to prevent certain behaviours and often, through them, to exclude particular groups.

## Main Text

### Page 9

A square may look exceptionally open in an aerial photograph: stone paving extends from the buildings into the distance, and the sightline follows orderly façades.

Shade, drinking water, toilets, and seats determine how long people can remain. An older person crosses the paving and soon searches for rest; a caregiver turns away with a child, while an outdoor worker must keep moving. The square receives passage, while daily life struggles to stay.

Facilities and relationships turn geometric emptiness into social use.

### Page 10

The opposite can also occur: what exists slowly consumes the common space.

Café tables move beyond their boundary, advertising boards enter the walking route, shared bicycles obstruct ramps, and temporary fences remain after an event. Each object has a reason. Each occupation can describe its benefit. Accumulated together, they leave anyone who does not pay with only a narrow line of passage.

Neglect damages public space, and excessive occupation also consumes the openness required for common use.

### Page 11

Jacobs's observations remind us that street vitality does not arise from removing people until order looks clean. Entrances, ordinary shops, activities at different hours, and people willing to remain give a street continuity of life.

Yet “eyes on the street” must also be handled carefully. Familiar attention can provide care, but it can also mark a stranger as suspicious. Informal order may supplement institutions; it cannot license discrimination, public shaming, or private punishment.

Genuine publicness does not require everyone to appear alike. It requires the capacity to meet difference without making expulsion of the vulnerable the first response.

### Page 12

Ostrom's work reminds us that shared use depends on rules.

Without boundaries, one large event may occupy a square indefinitely. When rules are made only by people far from the place, they may exclude the experience of vendors, children, wheelchair users, and nearby residents. Rules should identify what harms common use while giving affected people ways to speak, appeal, and revise.

This cannot be derived directly from the *Dao De Jing*. It does help a modern reader see that “leaving space” is not a passive condition after design ends. It is continuing work of governance.

### Page 13

Exclusionary design turns the problem over.

A bench still exists, but closely spaced dividers prevent an exhausted person from lying down. A sheltered ledge remains open, but raised metal makes it impossible for a body to approach. No sign says, “You may not stay.” The command has entered metal, angle, and distance.

Order and safety are real needs, and no public place can accommodate every behaviour. But when a city changes benches without providing housing, sanitation, or support, it often addresses not the problem itself but whether the problem remains visible.

### Page 14

Whether an opening is usable also depends upon the body that approaches it.

A door wide enough for a pedestrian may not allow a wheelchair to turn. Steps that barely register to a young adult may stop someone pushing a pram. An unshaded square may support gathering in mild weather and become unusable during extreme heat. Sound, lighting, language, and cost create thresholds that cannot always be seen.

Access is therefore not an abstract property. It asks which bodies can enter, how long they may remain, what price they must pay, and whether they can leave safely.

### Page 15

Public space requires clear responsibility and limits on permanent occupation by any person or group.

Good rules protect passage while permitting rest. They limit harm without treating whatever displeases an administrator as danger. They maintain equipment and cleanliness while allowing people affected by the rules to participate in review. No permanent plan fits every square.

An empty chair in a meeting raises the same question. If speaking order, specialised language, and differences in power keep a newcomer silent, the chair may be available while speech has no entrance. A space is not shared merely because it lacks a single owner. We must ask whether it serves only its most powerful users.

## Return to the Original

### Page 16

Return to Chapter Eleven. The hub opening is not an arbitrary hole; it admits the axle. A vessel's hollow is not abstract nothingness; it receives. The room within is not merely area inside walls; doors and windows connect it with human life.

“What is absent provides use” can therefore remind a modern city that an opening must actually form a passage. If a door opens only for certain bodies, a bench permits only brief stopping, and a square allows people to remain only when they pay, then the place may be formally empty while relationally occupied.

### Page 17

At dawn, a cleaner pushes a cart across the square, and its wheels leave a wet line on the paving. A vendor follows the line with a display rack and opens an awning; children run around the rack toward the fountain, while a passenger sets a bag beside the bench to wait for the first bus.

These actions enter the same open place in sequence, moving it among passage, rest, trade, play, and waiting.

Shared openness is closer to a promise that no single use may close it forever.

Walls define a boundary and doors allow entry. Rules protect common use, while common users must be able to return and revise the rules.

The real question of an open place is never only, “What is here?”

It is also: who may enter, who may remain, and who still has the power to let something new take place?
