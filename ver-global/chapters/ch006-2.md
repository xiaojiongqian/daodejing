# 第006-2章：留白的门

## 原文

### 第1页

河上公本将这一章题为“成象”：

谷神不死，是谓玄牝。
玄牝之门，是谓天地根。
绵绵若存，用之不勤。

### 第2页

白话说来：那像山谷一样空灵而有感应的生命作用，并不会消亡；它可称作幽深而能孕育万物的根源。万物由此出入，天地由此生发。它细微、连续，仿佛若有若无；顺着它来用，便不必把它逼到疲惫。

## 释义

### 第3页

古代注家从不同方向解释这几句话。王弼着眼于山谷中未被占据的部分：正因为没有固定形状，它才能容纳来水与回声。河上公则把“谷”读作养，把“用之不勤”落实到呼吸与养身：气息宜宽舒，不宜急疾劳顿。

两种读法关注的层面不同，却都反对逼迫。王弼谈生成需要余地，河上公谈身体不可久受急促之气。前者不必被改写成养生术，后者也不必被夸大成医学结论。

### 第4页

因此，“用之不勤”不是劝人逃离工作，也不是要求每天完成一项“安静任务”。它先处理一个很实际的问题：睡眠不足时仍靠咖啡继续，谈话尚未结束便急着答复，兴趣刚出现就立刻要求产出，这些做法能维持一时，却会改变我们与身体、他人和工作的关系。

休息为灵感准备条件，留白给答案留下形成时间。恢复时间一旦被挤占，注意力会变窄，判断会变急；亲近的人坐在旁边说完一段话，我们也可能只听见其中几句。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第5页

**成象**：河上公本为第六章所加的章题。它提示本章以谷、门、根等可感的形象，引向难以指认的生成之理。

**谷神**：并非山谷中的人格神。王弼以无形、处卑而能成物来解释它，强调“空”所具有的感应与成全。

**玄牝**：以幽深与生养的意象指向根源；不宜把它缩成某种固定的性别角色或神秘实体。

**绵绵若存，用之不勤**：细密地延续，仿佛存在而不可把握；使用时不急迫、不劳伤。河上公的注释特别把它联系到宽舒而不急促的呼吸。

## 画面描述

### 第6页

夜里，餐桌上的笔记本电脑仍亮着，日程表从早到晚排满会议。一杯水放在键盘旁，水面没有动过。视频会议结束后，母亲合上电脑，女儿站在走廊口问：“你现在能听我说一件事吗？”

母亲的手伸向手机，在碰到屏幕前停下。她把手机翻面，推到桌角，再将椅子转向女儿：“你慢慢说。”女儿看见她的双手都留在桌面，便拉开椅子坐下。

窗外传来车声，屋里保留着日常响动。母亲等在桌边，女儿按自己的速度说出第一句话。

## 正文

### 第7页

许多疲惫不只来自事情困难，也来自我们不再允许事情结束。升职以后，日历排得更满；休假时仍逐条回复工作消息；家人刚说起烦恼，我们便给出建议，仿佛迟几秒回答就算失职。

选择确实多了，权衡的时间却变少了。人可以在一天内处理许多事项，同时没有认真确认其中哪几件值得继续。

### 第8页

在职场里，“用之太勤”未必表现为加班。会议刚开始，负责人便宣布结论；有人提出疑问，主持者说稍后再谈，下一张幻灯片随即出现。会议按时结束，决定也已记录，却没有人确认那个疑问后来由谁处理。

给讨论留时间，并不是无限延长会议。可以是在提案之后停半分钟，可以指定一人陈述反对理由，也可以把尚无答案的问题写进下一次议程。停顿若没有后续，只是拖延；停顿若让异议进入决定，才有实际作用。

### 第9页

家庭里也是如此。一个不善表达的人说到一半停下来，未必是在拒绝交流。他可能正在找词，也可能在判断接下来的话是否安全。倾听者若立刻接管谈话，给出的建议再聪明，也可能答错了问题。

散步时偶尔不带耳机，午后留半小时不排任务，与亲近的人约定“这件事明天再回答”，都只是小动作。它们不保证顿悟，却能让人听见疲惫、犹豫和尚未说清的需要，而不是立即用下一项安排盖过去。

### 第10页

1952年，约翰·凯奇的作品《4分33秒》由钢琴家大卫·都铎首演。都铎坐在钢琴前，在三个乐章规定的时段内没有弹奏琴键。观众于是听见座椅轻响、厅外的风、衣料摩擦和呼吸；这些声音原本一直存在，只是通常不被当作演出的一部分。

这不是说沉默比音乐高贵，也不是说任何停顿都具有艺术价值。作品所做的很具体：演奏者暂时不按下琴键，听众便不得不面对现场已有的声音，以及自己等待音乐开始时的不耐烦。

### 第11页

凯奇不是在重述老子，他的作品也不等于道家哲学。二者可以比较的地方只有一点：主动停止一种声音，会让其他声音被听见。

对习惯按产出衡量价值的人来说，停顿常需要说明用途。我们敬重立即交付的答案，却很少记录：那个人是否听完问题，是否检查过反例，是否在疲惫时推迟了不该仓促作出的决定。“用之不勤”要求注意这些过程。

## 回扣

### 第12页

“玄牝之门”不要求人遁入山中。它可以落实为几件日常的事：疲惫时不替自己追加一场无必要的会议；孩子停顿时不替他说完；同事提出异议后，把问题记下来并给出答复；休息时不继续用消息证明自己仍在工作。

“用之不勤”也不是一律少做。该完成的工作仍要完成，该承担的责任不能推给“留白”。它只是要求我们别把持续紧张当成尽责，别等到身体、关系和判断都已受损，才承认人需要恢复。

# Chapter 006-2: The Gate That Leaves Room

## Original Text

### Page 1

The Heshang Gong recension gives this chapter the title “Forming Images”:

The valley spirit never dies; this is called the mysterious female.\
The gate of the mysterious female is called the root of heaven and earth.\
Fine and unbroken, it seems to be there; draw upon it, and it is never wearied.

### Page 2

In plain language: the living efficacy of a valley-like emptiness does not perish. It may be called the deep, generative source from which things arise. Through it, the ten thousand things come and go, and heaven and earth take root. It continues delicately, almost as if it were not there; when we use it in keeping with its nature, we need not drive it into fatigue.

## Interpretation

### Page 3

Ancient commentators approached these lines from different directions. Wang Bi attended to the unoccupied part of a valley: because it has no fixed shape, it can receive water and return sound. Heshang Gong reads *valley* as nurture and connects “do not use it too diligently” with breath and care of the body: breathing should be spacious rather than hurried or strained.

The readings concern different levels, but both reject strain. Wang Bi writes of the room generation requires; Heshang Gong writes of a body that should not remain under hurried breath. The first need not become a breathing prescription, and the second should not be enlarged into a modern medical claim.

### Page 4

“Do not use it too diligently” is not an instruction to flee work, nor a demand to complete a daily task called calm. It begins with a practical question. We sleep too little and continue with caffeine; answer before a conversation has ended; require a new interest to produce something at once. Such habits may sustain us briefly, but they change our relation to the body, to other people, and to work.

Rest does not guarantee insight, and empty time does not automatically yield a good answer. But without recovery, attention narrows and judgment becomes hurried. Those closest to us also notice that although we are sitting beside them, we have not heard what they said.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 5

**Forming Images**: The title assigned to Chapter Six in the Heshang Gong recension. It points to the chapter’s use of graspable images—valley, gate, root—to approach a process of generation that cannot itself be pointed to.

**Valley spirit**: Not a personal god in a valley. Wang Bi reads it as the responsive and completing efficacy of what has no visible form and remains low.

**Mysterious female**: An image of depth and generation. It should not be reduced either to a fixed gender role or to a mystical object.

**Fine and unbroken; never wearied**: A continuation too subtle to grasp. Heshang Gong particularly connects the line with breathing that is spacious rather than hurried.

## Visual Description

### Page 6

At night, a laptop still glows on the dining table, its calendar filled from morning to evening. A glass of water beside the keyboard has not been touched. When the video call ends, a mother closes the computer. Her daughter stands in the hallway and asks, “Can you listen to something now?”

The mother reaches toward her phone and stops before touching the screen. She turns it face down, pushes it to the edge of the table, and turns her chair toward her daughter. “Take your time.” Seeing both of her mother's hands resting on the table, the child pulls out a chair and sits down.

Traffic is still audible outside; the room is not silent. This time, however, no one asks her to say the first sentence more quickly.

## Main Text

### Page 7

Much exhaustion comes not only from difficult work, but from our refusal to let anything end. After promotion, the calendar fills further. On holiday, we answer each work message. A family member begins to describe a worry, and we offer advice at once, as if a few seconds of delay would be negligence.

Our options have multiplied while the time for weighing them has diminished. A person may process many items in one day without deciding which of them deserves to continue.

### Page 8

At work, overuse does not always look like overtime. A meeting begins and the leader announces the conclusion. Someone raises a question; the chair says it can be discussed later, and the next slide appears. The meeting ends on time and the decision is recorded, but no one knows who will return to the question.

Leaving time for discussion does not mean allowing every meeting to continue indefinitely. It may mean pausing for thirty seconds after a proposal, assigning someone to state the strongest objection, or placing an unanswered question on the next agenda. A pause without follow-up is delay. A pause that allows dissent to alter a decision has a practical use.

### Page 9

The same is true at home. When someone who struggles to speak stops halfway through a sentence, the pause is not necessarily refusal. He may be looking for words or deciding whether the rest is safe to say. If the listener takes over at once, even intelligent advice may answer the wrong question.

An occasional walk without headphones, half an hour left unscheduled, or an agreement with someone close to answer tomorrow are small acts. They promise no revelation. They simply make fatigue, hesitation, and an unfinished need easier to notice before the next arrangement covers them.

### Page 10

In 1952, John Cage’s *4′33″* was premiered by the pianist David Tudor. Tudor sat at the piano and did not play the keys during the timed three movements. The audience heard seats creak, wind outside, fabric move, and people breathe. Those sounds had been present all along; they were simply not usually treated as part of the performance.

This does not mean silence is nobler than music, or that every pause possesses artistic value. The piece does something specific: the performer does not press the keys, and listeners must confront the sounds already present, along with their own impatience for the music to begin.

### Page 11

Cage was not restating Laozi, and his work is not a substitute for Daoist thought. The comparison is limited to one point: when one intended sound stops, other sounds can be heard.

For those accustomed to measuring worth by output, a pause often has to justify itself. We admire an immediate answer but rarely record whether the speaker heard the question, checked a counterexample, or postponed a decision that should not be made while exhausted. “Do not overwork it” asks us to notice those processes.

## Return to the Original

### Page 12

The gate of the mysterious female does not require retreat to the mountains. It can be practiced in ordinary decisions: do not add an unnecessary meeting when exhausted; do not finish a child’s sentence; record a colleague’s objection and answer it; stop using messages during rest to prove that work continues.

“Use it without overworking it” does not mean always doing less. Necessary work remains necessary, and responsibility cannot be handed to an attractive idea of empty space. The line asks us not to mistake permanent tension for devotion, and not to wait until body, relationship, and judgment have all been damaged before admitting the need for recovery.
