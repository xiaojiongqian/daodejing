# 第003-1章：无为而治

## 原文

### 第1页

无为而治。

### 第2页

不尚贤，使民不争；不贵难得之货，使民不为盗；不见可欲，使心不乱。

### 第3页

是以圣人治，虚其心，实其腹，弱其志，强其骨，常使民无知无欲，使夫知者不敢为也。为无为，则无不治。

## 释义

### 第4页

“无为而治”要求治理者收住强求的手，先察看事势，再让万物循其本性运行。

### 第5页

治理者停止抬高名位，百姓争逐名位的热度便会降低；社会停止炫示稀有财货，盗取它们的诱因也随之减弱；欲望少受挑动，人心便容易安定。

### 第6页

圣人治理时，先减轻民众受名利牵动的焦躁，再充实他们的基本生活；好胜之志收敛下来，身心力量便得到保存。百姓安于日用，权计失去煽动人心的支点。治理者依此行事，天下便有了安宁的根基。

## 术语与考据简注

### 第7页

**无为而治**：治理顺应事势，人的欲望受到节制。

**不尚贤**：不刻意拔高少数贤能作为统治象征，避免引发权力争斗和不平衡。

**无知无欲**：让民众保有平实的生活，名利欲望退居次位。

**为无为，则无不治**：以不争、不强、不夺的治理方式实施天下事，则没有治理不了的局面。

## 正文

### 第8页

“无为而治”的理路源于对人心和社会秩序的观察。

公元前202年，刘邦统一天下。但所谓“天下”，是秦末连年战争留下的巨大废墟：《史记·平准书》载，当时“自天子不能具钧驷，而将相或乘牛车，齐民无藏盖”——皇帝找不到四匹毛色一样的马拉车，将相只能坐牛车，百姓家无余粮。

面对此境，统治集团面临两种选择：一是沿袭秦制，以严刑峻法、大兴土木来展示新朝威权；

二是减轻干预，让社会休养生息。最终，刘邦及其后继者大体选择了第二条路——采纳陆贾“逆取顺守，文武并用”的建议，并以黄老思想指导汉初政治。

### 第9页

*萧规曹随与“不扰民”*

汉初无为而治的纲领，可以从几个相连的政策选择中看见：

萧何的立法：《汉书·刑法志》说他“捃摭秦法，取其宜于时者，作律九章”。他沿用秦法，同时选择、增补。观察汉初刑政，还要结合其后减轻刑罚与徭役的政策。

曹参的“治大国若烹小鲜”：萧何去世后，曹参继任。他每日饮酒，不理细务，对下属“择郡国吏木诎于文辞，重厚长者，即召除为丞相史”——专挑那些不善言辞、朴实厚道的人做官，排斥那些巧言令色、精明刻薄之辈。

汉惠帝责问，曹参答：“陛下垂拱，参等守职，遵而勿失，不亦可乎？”（《史记·曹相国世家》）这便是 “萧规曹随” 的典故——前任定下的好制度，后任绝不为了显示自己而妄加更改。

### 第10页

文帝的“除肉刑”与“减田租”：汉文帝更是将无为推至极致：废除了残暴的肉刑（断足、割鼻等）；田租由秦时的“十税一”降至“三十税一”，甚至连续十二年免收全国田租；徭役减为每丁三年服役一次。

汉初的“无为”把克制落实在税赋、徭役和日常行政上：

“我无为而民自化”：朝廷不规定百姓必须种什么、如何生活，民间商业、手工业在无干涉下自发繁荣。

### 第11页

“我好静而民自正”：朝廷在若干时期对匈奴采取和亲，边境冲突虽未停止，整体上仍避免了持续的大规模征伐；大兴土木也较秦时有所收敛。

“我无事而民自富”：国家减少征税、减少徭役，民间财富自然积累。

其结果，便是史称的“文景之治”。

《汉书·食货志》描绘道：“太仓之粟陈陈相因，充溢露积于外，至腐败不可食。”国库串钱的绳子都腐烂了，钱币散落无法计数。

### 第12页

圣人治国时，先让名位与欲望退出日常生活的中心。“虚其心”使私欲造成的执念逐渐松开。

### 第13页

“实其腹”把粮食和生计送进家庭，“弱其志”让过度野心收敛，“强其骨”则使民众保有体力与精神。三者相接，治理从安顿生活开始。

### 第14页

百姓沉静而知足，权谋者便难以用名利挑起风波，也难以驱使众人追随自己的算计。

### 第15页

因此，这种“无为”的治理通过节制欲望、降低纷争来形成秩序，使天下逐步归于安宁。

## 回扣

### 第16页

本章强调的是治国之术，应当顺应人情和社会自然律。若以高压和名利引导民心，便会生出争夺、盗窃与纷乱之象。

### 第17页

这里所说的治理，是以克制干预来保留平和与自足。让百姓少受知识炫示和欲望刺激的扰动，也让擅弄权计者不敢妄为，秩序才可能逐渐安定。

# Chapter 003-1: Governing by Non-Action

## Original Text

### Page 1

Governing by non-action.

### Page 2

Do not exalt the worthy, so that the people will not compete. Do not value rare goods, so that the people will not steal. Do not display what is desirable, so that their hearts will not be disturbed.

### Page 3

Therefore the Sage governs by emptying their hearts, filling their bellies, weakening their wills, strengthening their bones, and always making the people without knowledge and desire so that those who know do not dare act. Act without action, and there will be nothing unruled.

## Interpretation

### Page 4

“Governing by non-action” is not about doing nothing; it is about ruling without forcing, allowing things to follow their natural course.

### Page 5

If the ruler does not exalt the talented, the people will not fight for position; if rare treasures are not prized, there is no motive for theft; if desire is not paraded, minds remain calm.

### Page 6

When the Sage governs, he clears restless hearts and secures their daily needs, weakens excessive ambition and strengthens the body. He keeps the people free from excess knowledge and desire, so those who would scheme do not dare to act. By doing nothing, everything is taken care of.

## Key Terms and Notes

### Page 7

**Governing by non-action**: Not passive neglect, but ruling without intrusive control and allowing natural order to prevail.

**Do not exalt the worthy**: Avoid elevating a few talents as symbols of power, which can invite rivalry and social imbalance.

**Without knowledge and desire**: Not ignorance in a pejorative sense, but preserving a simple, untroubled state of mind.

**Act without action**: Manage affairs in a way that avoids contest, force, and seizure, so no unresolved problems remain.

## Main Text

### Page 8

The reasoning behind “governing by non-action” begins with an observation of human motives and social order.

In 202 BCE, Liu Bang unified a realm left exhausted by years of war. The Records of the Grand Historian describes an economy so depleted that even the emperor could not find four horses of the same color for his carriage, ministers sometimes traveled by ox cart, and ordinary households had no reserves.

The new rulers faced a choice: continue Qin-style displays of authority through severe law and large projects, or reduce interference and allow society to recover. Liu Bang and his successors largely chose the latter, following Lu Jia’s advice to combine civil and military means and drawing on Huang-Lao thought in early Han government.

### Page 9

*Following Established Rules and Avoiding Disturbance*

The Book of Han says that Xiao He drew from Qin law, selected what suited the time, and made the Nine-Chapter Code. This was not a simple abolition of Qin law; continuity, selection, and later reform coexisted. After Xiao He’s death, Cao Can did not rewrite policies merely to display his own ability. He selected steady, plainspoken officials and resisted constant administrative interference.

When Emperor Hui questioned this approach, Cao Can answered that the emperor could remain composed while ministers fulfilled their duties and preserved what already worked. The episode gave rise to the saying “Cao followed Xiao’s rules.”

### Page 10

Emperor Wen carried restraint into taxation and punishment. He abolished several forms of mutilating punishment, lowered the land tax, and reduced demands for compulsory labor.

Early Han “non-action” was therefore not simple neglect. The court intervened less in how people farmed, traded, and arranged their daily lives, leaving households and local economies room to recover.

### Page 11

The court also limited large projects and, during several periods, used the heqin policy toward the Xiongnu. Frontier conflict did not cease, but prolonged large-scale campaigns were generally avoided. Lower taxes and labor demands allowed private wealth to accumulate.

The result was later remembered as the “Rule of Wen and Jing.” The Book of Han describes grain overflowing from state granaries and strings binding stored coins rotting with age.

### Page 12

When the Sage rules, he first empties the hearts of the people. “Emptying the heart” is not making them idle, but removing the grip of selfish desire.

### Page 13

To “fill their bellies” is to secure everyone’s basic livelihood; to “weaken their wills” is not to destroy ambition, but to prevent ambition from swelling beyond measure; to “strengthen their bones” is to make the people physically robust and mentally resilient.

### Page 14

When the people are calm and content, those with clever schemes do not dare to act. No one wants to stir up trouble in such a stable environment.

### Page 15

Thus, governing through non-action builds order by reducing desire and conflict, allowing the realm to settle through ordinary life.

## Return to the Original

### Page 16

This chapter emphasizes the art of governance: it should follow human nature and natural law. If a ruler leads by pressure and desire, conflict, theft, and disorder will arise.

### Page 17

The rule described here preserves peace and sufficiency through restraint. When people are less disturbed by displays of cleverness and desire, and schemers do not dare act recklessly, stability has room to emerge.
